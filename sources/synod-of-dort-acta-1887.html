Synod of Dort — Acta of Handelingen der Nationale Synode (Donner ed., 1887)

Acta of Handelingen der Nationale Synode, in den naam Onzes Heeren Jezus Christus, gehouden door autoriteit der Hoogmogende Heeren Staten-Generaal der Vereenigde Nederlanden te Dordrecht, ten jare 1618 en 1619. Hier komen ook bij de volledige Beoordeelingen (“judgments / assessments”) van de Vijf Artikelen (“Five Articles”) en de Post-Acta of Nahandelingen (“after-proceedings”). Modern Dutch spelling after the original Low-Dutch edition, under the supervision of J. H. Donner and S. A. van den Hoorn. Leiden: D. Donner, 1887 (Anna’s Archive / OCLC 29128501; University of Minnesota library scan).

This is the official published record of the Nationale Synode (“National Synod”) at Dordrecht (Dort), 1618–1619 — the Republic’s great church assembly called by the Staten-Generaal (“States-General”) to end the Remonstrant controversy over predestination and grace. Foreign Reformed theologians sat with Dutch provincial deputies; the Remonstrants were cited as defendants; the assembly produced the Canones (“Canons”) of Dort, reviewed the Belijdenis (“Confession”) and Catechismus, and then continued in Post-Acta church-order work. For this wiki it sits beside Motley’s Barneveld narrative (1609–1623) as the primary ecclesiastical/political document of the same crisis that ended with johan-van-oldenbarnevelt’s fall and the triumph of the Contra-Remonstrant / Orangist settlement under maurice-of-nassau.

The scan is 1,014 page-files concatenated as ~100,300 lines (~4.0 MB) in wiki/raw/synod-of-dort-acta-1887.txt.

Completeness

Full Donner modernization: States-General dedication (1620), long historical Voorrede, numbered Zittingen (“sessions”) of the Acta, the body of foreign and Dutch Beoordeelingen on the Five Articles, the Synod’s doctrinal decision / Canons material, the French national synod’s later approval (Alès, 1620), the Post-Acta, and closing registers. Not a modern scholarly commentary — the text speaks in the Synod’s and States’ own terms.

Contents

States-General dedication (The Hague, 27 March 1620)

The States-General address the “highest and most powerful monarchs and kings of Christendom,” princes, counts, cities, and magistrates. They argue that the Republic’s foundation is eenheid (“unity”), and that unity rests on true religion. While an external enemy pressed, the provinces stood firm; once the Truce removed that fear, internal religious strife nearly purchased ruin. Church and Republic can scarcely be separated in this life. They therefore summoned a Synod at Dordrecht — Holland’s capital for this purpose — and, especially on the advice of James I of Great Britain and with the counsel of the Prince of Orange, invited famous foreign theologians alongside Dutch professors, ministers, and elders. Those deputies bound themselves by oath to judge only by God’s Word and conscience; they decided the long quarrel over the bounds of human will and divine grace under the name of praedestinatie (“predestination”). The published Canones, signed by the members, and a careful review of Confession and Catechism are offered as proof of agreement. The States publish the Acta under their authority and beg allied princes to remember this concord and to plant God’s kingdom after their example.

Voorrede aan de Gereformeerde Kerken (Preface to the Reformed Churches)

A long narrative preface (printed pages III–XXXVIII in the edition’s own numbering) explains why printing the Canons alone was not enough: many still refuse to learn from the Synodal proceedings how the Remonstranten (“Remonstrants”) were handled, and the Remonstrants themselves cannot be trusted to publish a faithful account. The States therefore ordered the Acta transcribed from the public writings.

The preface then walks the origin and progress of the Dutch disputes that made the Synod necessary: the teaching and death of Arminius, the rise of the Remonstrantie (“Remonstrance”) and the party named from it, Uitenbogaard, Vorstius at Leiden, provincial conferences over the Vijf Artikelen, second Remonstrances, clashes over whether provincial or national church judgment should decide, and the long refusal or delay of a national synod while the Republic’s peace turned into internal war of pulpits and parties. It ends by pointing the reader not only to the Canons but to the full judgments of the theologians on the Five Articles, so that the Scripture grounds of the decision can be seen.

Opening sessions — composition, oath, foreign guests (Zittingen 1–22)

De Eerste Zitting opens the assembly at Dordrecht. Commissioners of the States-General, Dutch professors, ministers, and elders take their places; foreign theologians are formally conducted in and seated according to the dignity of the powers that sent them. Proceedings are to be held in Latin for the foreigners’ sake. Balthazar Lydius, minister at Dordrecht, opens with prayer and oration recalling Reformation deliverance, Orange’s wars, and the bitterness of peace that followed — wild grapes on the planted vine — and asks the Spirit to preside so that the “mouth of the Lord” may be asked over the disputes.

Early sessions establish credentials, rules of order, the Presidency (including Bogerman), the role of the States’ commissioners, and the reception of foreign colleges (English, Palatine, Hessian, Swiss, Bremen, Emden, Geneva, and others named throughout). Catechising, printing abuses, Bible translation questions, and provincial church business appear before the Remonstrant cause dominates the floor.

Remonstrants cited and heard (from about Zitting 23)

From December 1618 the Remonstrants appear as gedaagden (“defendants / those cited”). They request the presence and counsel of Grevinchovius and Goulartius; the States’ commissioners refuse to reverse prior church depositions but allow limited private assistance if it does not delay the Synod. Utrecht Remonstrants are pressed to choose whether they sit as judges under the Synodal oath or join the defendants as advocates of the same cause.

Simon Episcopius, Leiden professor of theology, speaks for the cited Remonstrants without first asking leave: an oration defending their stand against opinions they held incompatible with God’s glory, Christ’s love and merits, the Word, the Spirit’s ministry, the sacraments, and the Christian’s office — and against premature schism over the Five Articles before a synod had judged. Later sessions record repeated summonses, written conditions, comparisons to Trent and other refused judgments, and the Remonstrants’ resistance to submitting fully to this Synod’s authority while still under the same magistrates. Around the later procedural climax they are sent away; the assembly continues without them as party on the floor.

Doctrinal decision — Canons and first heads of doctrine

After the Remonstrants’ departure, the Synod moves from citation politics to drafting and reading its decision. Sessions in the 130s–140s prepare the Canones. The extract includes the headed first chapter of doctrine, Van de Goddelijke Verkiezing en Verwerping (“On Divine Election and Reprobation”), and surrounding Voorrede and concluding Synodal judgment language. Confession and Catechism review, promised in the States’ dedication, is part of the same settlement: the received forms are judged agreeable to Scripture and to be left unchanged to posterity.

Beoordeelingen van de Vijf Artikelen (Judgments on the Five Articles)

A very large middle block prints the volledige Beoordeelingen — the written judgments of the foreign and Dutch colleges on the Remonstrants’ Five Articles and related heads (election, the extent of Christ’s death, free will and grace, perseverance, and related distinctions such as “acquisition and appropriation”). Colleges speak in turn: Great Britain, the Palatinate, Hesse, Switzerland, Bremen, Emden, the Dutch professors, Gelderland, South and North Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Friesland, Overijssel, Groningen, Drenthe, the Walloon churches, and others. These judgments are the working papers behind the Canons: argument from Scripture and Reformed consensus, college by college, before the common Synodal voice is sealed.

French approval (Alès, 1620) and related instruments

Near the end of the main Acta block the edition prints the Oordeel en uitspraak of the National Synod of the Reformed Churches of France at Alez (Alès) in the Cévennes, 6 October 1620, with the form of oath of approbation of Dort’s Canons — showing how the Dutch decision was received in a sister Reformed national synod.

Post-Acta / Nahandelingen (from about Zitting 155)

After the doctrinal settlement, the Post-Acta (also called Na-handelingen) continue the Synod’s ordinary and unfinished church business: further sessions numbered from the 155th onward, with a short address to the reader naming editors/compilers such as Winandus Schulius, Cornelius Triglandius, Cornelius Vergeer, and Petrus Hollebeeck. This is the administrative after-work — classes, provincial synods, discipline, and implementation — not a second doctrinal debate.

Registers

Closing alphabetical and topical registers point back into the Voorrede and the judgments (election, reprobation, perseverance, regeneration, will of fallen man, and the colleges that treated each head), ending with EINDE and a short list of printer’s corrections.

Coverage for this wiki

For the Dutch Republic wiki, this Acta is especially useful for:

  • Religion and politics as one crisis — the States-General’s claim that church discord endangers the Republic; James I and Orange as sponsors of a national synod; magistrates’ commissioners in the hall (dedication + opening sessions). Pair with religion-and-politics, dutch-republic-power-structure, and maurice-of-nassau.
  • Remonstrant / Contra-Remonstrant conflict — Arminius, Uitenbogaard, Vorstius, Episcopius, Five Articles, citation as defendants, refusal of Synod authority (Voorrede + sessions ~23–57). Essential primary contrast to Motley’s Barneveld narrative and to johan-van-oldenbarnevelt.
  • Canons of Dort — election and reprobation chapter; Confession and Catechism review; signed Canones as “handschriften der Kerken” (dedication + Canons block).
  • International Reformed network — English, Palatine, Hessian, Swiss, Bremen, Emden, Genevan, and French (Alès) participation and judgments.
  • Church order after the purge — Post-Acta sessions for how the settlement was to be applied in classes and provinces.
  • Primary language of the period — unity, true religion, predestination, Remonstrants, Canons, Belijdenis, Catechismus — in the Synod’s and States’ own Dutch (modernized spelling).

OCR Quality

Good readable Dutch prose for a late-nineteenth-century modernization of a seventeenth-century official print. Running headers (“HANDELINGEN DER NATIONALE SYNODE,” session labels) repeat often; some page numbers and the imprint year are OCR-garbled (title leaf shows “1008” for the Donner date; use 1887 from the archival record). Early University of Minnesota binder/stamp leaves are empty or nearly so. Body text of dedication, Voorrede, sessions, judgments, and Post-Acta is continuous enough for quotation with light normalisation.

About the Author

David W. Davies was a historian of the book and printing. Acknowledgements in the preface mention assistance from Professor Dr P. Geyl (University of Utrecht), Dr A. Kessen (University of Leyden), and support from the International University Foundation, which enabled Davies to work in the Netherlands. The spelling ‘Elsevier’ is adopted as most common in the seventeenth century; ‘Leyden’ is used for English readers.

Scope and Approach

Davies writes in the preface: “In the following pages an attempt has been made to give the essential facts of the history of the Elsevier family, and to show the relations of the printers to the world around them. Printing and publishing history is sometimes written as personal reminiscence, as aesthetic or technical criticism, or as a guide for book collectors. There is something to be said for treating it as a phase of economic or social history, and this treatment has been attempted here.”

The book covers the period from 1580 (when Louis Elsevier fled Antwerp for Leyden during the Dutch Revolt) to 1712 (the death of the last Elsevier printer, Daniel Elsevier of Amsterdam) — 132 years and four generations of the family.

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

Chapter 1 — The First of the Elseviers (p. 1) Louis Elsevier was born at Louvain in 1546/47, son of a printer called “Hans of Louvain.” Louvain University had once been the glory of Netherlands humanism (Erasmus, Vives, Adrian VI, Thierry Martens — the “Aldus of the Netherlands”). Louis married at 18 (1563) and moved with his father to Antwerp (c. 1565), both taking jobs with Christophe Plantin — Hans as a pressman, Louis folding and collating signatures. The chapter provides extensive background on Antwerp’s prosperity (250 ships at the docks, the Venetian envoy said it transacted as much in a fortnight as Venice in a year) and its printing industry (2,230 of 4,000+ Netherlands books published 1500–1540 came from Antwerp). It then details the Revolt of the Netherlands — the Spanish fiscal crisis, the rise of Calvinism, the image-smashing (Beeldenstorm, 1566), the arrival of the Duke of Alva (1567), Louis’s conversion to Protestantism and flight from Antwerp. Louis fled to Liège (1568), then Wesel, then Douai (where he did work for Plantin by post). The chapter gives a clear summary of the Revolt’s causes: alien Spanish rule, commercial frustration, religious militancy, and noble ambitions.

Chapter 2 — Louis Elsevier at Leyden (p. 19) Louis arrived in Leyden in 1581, listed in the census as “a binder of Louvain” with one assistant. The Leyden book trade was fiercely competitive — rivals included Jan Orlers (printer and historian of Leyden), Hendrik van Haestens (author of a history of the Siege of Ostend), and the official University printers (Willem Silvius, then Christopher Plantin’s grandson). Louis won the appointment as beadle of the University (a minor official who kept order in lectures and assisted professors), which gave him a foothold. Despite the Revolt, Leyden’s University flourished, attracting scholars from across Europe. Louis gradually built a book business on the side, binding and selling to students and professors. He published his own first book in 1586 (an edition of a Latin Classic, date uncertain) but remained primarily a binder and beadle. The chapter provides a detailed picture of the Leyden academic market and Louis’s slow, steady establishment.

Chapter 3 — The Elseviers at The Hague, Utrecht, and Leyden (p. 34) Louis had nine children; fourteen descendants became booksellers or printers. This chapter traces the family branches outside the main Leyden line:

  • The Hague shop (founded c. 1590 by Louis’s second son, also named Louis): Served the legal and government market — the States General, the Court of Holland. Lasted until c. 1665, passing through eight Elsevier hands.
  • Utrecht branch (founded 1603 by Jan Elsevier, another son): A small operation; Jan’s son also worked in Livorno, Italy for a time.
  • Leyden succession: Louis was succeeded at his death (1617) by his grandson Abraham and nephew Bonaventura (a partnership that would become the most famous Elsevier firm).

Chapter 4 — Abraham and Bonaventura Elsevier (p. 53) The partnership (1622–1652) was the golden age of the Elseviers. Abraham handled the presswork, Bonaventura the business. They published nearly half of all Elsevier books ever issued (in 132 years of the firm’s existence), and were the best typographers of the dynasty. The chapter provides extensive Leyden economic data drawn from Nicolaas Posthumus’s statistics: from 1581 to 1622 Leyden’s population grew 268% (12,144 → 44,745); from 1622 to 1652 it grew 41% (to ~63,000). The cost of living rose 75% between 1580–84 and 1620–24, but only 43% between 1620–24 and 1650–54 — a period of stable prosperity. Leyden’s cloth industry also matured. The Elseviers cultivated authors and scholars — including Daniel Heinsius (professor and poet, their most important author), Joost van den Vondel, and Gerardus Vossius. They published in Latin, French, and Dutch, with a speciality in classical texts, legal works, and French literature.

Chapter 5 — The Successors to Abraham and Bonaventura Elsevier (p. 76) After Abraham and Bonaventura died in 1652, the Leyden business passed to their sons Johannes and Daniel. Johannes was erratic and sold his share; Daniel ran the shop until his death in 1680, producing fine editions but with declining output. The chapter covers the competition from imitators — at least seven other Leyden printers plus Blaeu and Janssonius in Amsterdam produced Elzevir-style duodecimos. It also covers the declining market for Latin classics as Dutch readers shifted to vernacular works.

Chapter 6 — The Amsterdam House: Louis and Daniel Elsevier (p. 97) The Amsterdam branch was founded by Louis Elsevier (a grandson of the original Louis) and later run by his cousin Daniel. This was the last significant Elsevier operation. The Amsterdam Elseviers built connections with French scholars and publishers (Menage, Balzac) and issued important works by Descartes, Galileo, and Hobbes. The chapter gives a picture of Amsterdam as the Dutch Golden Age’s commercial and intellectual capital. Daniel Elsevier died in 1680 — after him, the family name survived only in memory.

Chapter 7 — The Elsevierian World (p. 122) A synthetic chapter situating the Elseviers in their broader European context. Davies describes the rise of absolutist states (Prussia, France under Louis XIV, Russia under the Romanoffs, Scandinavia) and contrasts this with the decentralized, urban, mercantile character of the Dutch Republic. The Elseviers flourished because the Dutch Republic offered: (1) freedom from centralized censorship, (2) a wealthy bourgeois market for books, (3) excellent transport and distribution networks, (4) a concentration of scholars and universities. The chapter discusses the nature of the Republic of Letters — the pan-European community of scholars communicating via letters and books — and the Elseviers’ role as its publishers. It covers censorship (the Elseviers sometimes printed books forbidden elsewhere, including works by Descartes), the book fair at Frankfurt, and the economics of international distribution.

Chapter 8 — The Elseviers as Printers and Publishers (p. 142) A thematic analysis of their output:

  • Subject breakdown: Religion/theology first, then law/politics, then classics, then French belles lettres.
  • Proofreading: The Elseviers were not scholars themselves (unlike Aldus or the Estiennes). They depended on authors and hired proofreaders. The notable exception was Johan de Laet — a director of the WIC, Synod of Dordrecht member, who edited their “Republics” series (descriptions of various countries) and feuded with Grotius about the origin of American Indians.
  • The duodecimo format: The Elseviers’ most famous innovation — small, cheap, portable editions of classics and contemporary works. The format was wildly imitated: Rahir’s catalogue lists ~1,350 imitation Elsevier duodecimos vs only ~675 genuine ones.
  • Imitators: At Leyden (Jan Maire, Philip de Croy, Severyn Mathys, Frans Hacke, etc.), Amsterdam (Johan Blaeu, Johannes Janssonius, Abraham Wolfgang), The Hague, Gouda, Brussels, Paris, Rouen, Liège.
  • Influence: Elsevier duodecimos inspired the Barbou reprint series (1743–1810, 76 volumes), and were cited in English wills (Sir Thomas Browne buried with his Elzevir Horace). Addison satirized the Elzevir-collecting mania in Spectator (Tom Folio). Their reputation for typographic excellence persisted through the 19th century (Timperley, 1842, praised their “clearness, delicacy and perfect equality of the characters”). The chapter closes with Andrew Lang’s wry observation that novelists wrote of “priceless little Elzevirs” without knowing why.

Bibliographical Note (p. 157) Lists the standard bibliographies: Willems’ Les Elzevier (1880), Berghman’s studies of typographical ornaments, Rahir’s catalogue, and other secondary works. The detailed technical bibliography is not reproduced since Willems, Berghman, and Rahir have already covered it.

Key Excerpts

Preface (v–vi)

In the following pages an attempt has been made to give the essential facts of the history of the Elsevier family, and to show the relations of the printers to the world around them. Printing and publishing history is sometimes written as personal reminiscence, as aesthetic or technical criticism, or as a guide for book collectors. There is something to be said for treating it as a phase of economic or social history, and this treatment has been attempted here…

So much assistance from friends has been sought and given that the story presented is a mosaic of the learning of others. The writer has reserved for himself only the special province of errors and omissions, and hereby lays claim to all such as may be found.

Louis Elsevier at Antwerp with Plantin (p. 3)

The city had ample printing and publishing facilities; indeed, in the opinion of conservatives, facilities were too ample. Plantin was the most famous printer of the town, but other excellent printers, Willem Silvius, Nutius, Bellerus and Johann Steels, also worked there. Of the somewhat more than four thousand books appearing in the Netherlands between 1500 and 1540, two thousand two hundred and thirty were published at Antwerp.

The name of the pressman, ‘Hans of Louvain called Helsevire,’ appears numerous times in the records of workmen employed at the Plantin house after 1565. The evidence of Louis Elsevier’s connections with Plantin are not so numerous, but in the Plantin-Moretus Museum there is a large folio manuscript volume written in Plantin’s hand entitled Book of the Workmen employed by the Printing House. For the year 1565 there are entries which read substantially as follows:

21 January, Loys Helsevire for 53 reams, gathered and collated — 1 florins 29 January, Louis Helsevire for 69 reams of Les Secrets d’Alexis Piemontoys and of the Epistolae Ciceronis, etc. — 1.14½ florins …

The connection thus formed with Plantin by Elsevier was probably never completely broken during the lifetime of the former.

Antwerp Crisis and the Flight (pp. 4–7)

There were political clouds on the horizon, and as a matter of fact, many of these clouds were bursting over Antwerp during the first years of Louis’ stay in the city… Louis Elsevier was himself converted to Protestantism at Antwerp… In 1566 the Prince of Orange and his disaffected fellow aristocrats rode in and took command of Antwerp… The Calvinists were emboldened, embarked on an orgy of sacking Catholic churches, and an epidemic of desecration ran through the Netherlands. In the following year there was an armed rising, and this brought to the town the terrible Duke of Alva and his Spanish troops, bent on vengeance. As Alva came into the city many Protestants fled, among them Louis Elsevier and his family.

The Economic Context of the Abraham & Bonaventura Partnership (pp. 53–54)

From 1581 to 1622… the Leyden population climbed from twelve thousand one hundred and forty-four to forty-four thousand seven hundred and forty-five, an increase of more than two hundred and sixty-eight per cent. During the period of the partnership population increased forty-one per cent, standing at about sixty-three thousand in 1652… Prices in the city of everyday necessities such as wheat, oats, rye, herring, beef, beans, cheese, butter, peat, soap, paper, and hay were sixty-three per cent higher in the years 1620 to 1624 than they had been in the years 1580 to 1584, whereas in the years 1650 to 1654 they had increased only thirty per cent over the years 1620 to 1624.

The Imitation Problem (p. 148)

In the catalogue of his collection Rahir listed about thirteen hundred and fifty imitations of Elsevier duodecimos, as against only about six hundred and seventy-five actually printed by Elseviers. Not only format, but Elsevier ornaments, aegipans, grotesques, hollyhocks, and spheres, were reproduced with varying degrees of skill. At Leyden in 1651 there were eight presses in operation (in addition to that of Abraham and Bonaventura), and at least seven of the eight printers… printed imitation Elseviers. At Amsterdam almost all printers were their imitators, including Johan Blaeu, Johannes Janssonius, Johannes J. van Waesberghe, and Abraham Wolfgang.

The Enduring Influence (pp. 149–152)

At Leyden in 1651 there were eight presses… Sir Thomas Browne… desired by his will that ‘on my coffin when in the grave I desire may be deposited in its leather case or coffin my Elzevir’s Horace’… Nearly a hundred years later, in 1776, the Abbe de Fontenai observed the Elseviers ‘have made Holland celebrated for printing, through an elegance of type which the most famous printers of Europe have never been able to attain, either before or since’… Addison’s Tom Folio ‘had greater esteem for Aldus and Elzevir, than for Virgil and Horace.‘

Relevance to the Dutch Republic Knowledge Base

This source is directly relevant to several existing and potential wiki pages:

  1. The Elsevier family (new entity): Four generations of printers (1580–1712) who defined Dutch Golden Age publishing — their Louvain origins, Plantin connection, Leyden establishment, and Amsterdam branch. Could be an entity page.

  2. The Leyden book trade (links to dutch-knowledge-culture): The Elseviers were at the centre of the University of Leyden’s academic publishing. Their competitors included Orlers, Haestens, Silvius, and the University printers. The chapter on Abraham & Bonaventura gives detailed economic data about Leyden’s growth.

  3. Antwerp printing industry (links to dutch-knowledge-culture, antwerp): The chapter on Louis Elsevier’s Antwerp years (c. 1565–1568) provides rich detail on the Plantin workshop and Antwerp’s dominance of Netherlands publishing (2,230 books out of 4,000+).

  4. The duodecimo revolution: The Elseviers’ small-format cheap classics were the 17th-century equivalent of paperbacks — wildly imitated, widely distributed. This connects to the broader story of the commercialization of knowledge.

  5. The Republic of Letters: The chapter “The Elsevierian World” situates the family within the pan-European community of scholars, discussing the Frankfurt book fair, international distribution, and the Elseviers’ role in publishing Descartes and other controversial authors.

  6. Economic history of the Dutch Golden Age: Davies uses Serrurier/Posthumus price and population data for Leyden, showing the stabilization after the explosive growth of 1581–1622 — valuable economic context.

  7. Censorship and freedom of the press: The Elseviers printed works that were banned elsewhere (Descartes, forbidden French literature), exploiting the Dutch Republic’s relative freedom. This connects to the broader theme of Dutch tolerance.

  8. Blaeu connection: Johan Blaeu is listed as one of the Amsterdam printers who imitated Elsevier duodecimos — cross-reference with the existing joan-blaeu entity and dutch-cartography-and-atlases.

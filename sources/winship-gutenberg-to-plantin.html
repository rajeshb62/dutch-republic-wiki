About the Author

George Parker Winship (1871–1952) was a prominent American librarian and bibliographer. He served as librarian of the Harry Elkins Widener Memorial Library at Harvard and was a noted authority on early printing and rare books. The book was heavily influenced by Alfred W. Pollard of the British Museum, with Winship acknowledging Pollard as responsible for most of the statements in the outline.

Scope and Content

The book covers the first 150 years of printing in Europe (c. 1450–1600), organized chronologically and thematically. Winship describes it as “an outline aims to be a statement of facts which have in most cases long been established, with as much of the newer conclusions drawn from these facts as seems to the author reasonably certain of eventual acceptance. Controversial details have usually been ignored.”

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

Chapter 1 — The Invention of Typography, 1440–1456 (p. 3) Gutenberg, a Mainz native, experimented in Strassburg from the 1430s. Banker Johann Fust financed production of the 42-line Bible (1450–1456), managed by Peter Schoeffer as foreman — six presses, 2–3 workmen each, plus type-casters, paper handlers, correctors, and binders. Fust sued Gutenberg in 1455, seizing the Bible type. Gutenberg’s only other known book is the Catholicon (1460), an encyclopaedic dictionary printed in a compact type. The chapter also describes the Indulgences of 1454–55 and Gutenberg’s cost-reduction experiments (filing type bodies to fit 42 lines instead of 40, saving ~5% on paper).

Chapter 2 — The Period of Development, 1460–1480 (p. 9) Fust & Schoeffer’s Psalter (1457) — the second printed book — used intricate two-color woodcut initials (red and blue), a technical feat experts still debate. The 1462 Bible (in a smaller type) introduced the first printer’s mark (coats of arms in the colophon). Strassburg rivals Mentelin and Rusch followed, possibly with pre-perfection knowledge from Gutenberg’s Strassburg years. The chapter also covers: the 36-line Bible (an earlier type but printed after the 42-line), the 1448 Calendar fragment (Gutenberg’s earliest experiment), the Donatus school-book fragments, and the emergence of Pfister at Bamberg with the first illustrated German-language books (1461–62).

Chapter 3 — The Spread of Printing Throughout Germany (p. 15) The 1462 sacking of Mainz dispersed printers across Germany. Cologne specialized in small quarto textbooks (16–24 pages) for its university — over 1,400 titles from at least 9 presses, many unidentifiable. A chronological list traces the spread: Augsburg (1468), Nuremberg (1470), Ulm (1473), Lübeck (1474), and many more. The chapter describes itinerant printers who carried only a hand-mould, casting new local-appropriate type-faces at each stop, and the economics of setting up a press (paper = ~50% of cost, ~2 years to produce first book). The Augsburg wood-engravers’ guild forced printer Gunther Zainer to employ local engravers, making Augsburg a centre for illustrated vernacular books.

Chapter 4 — The Establishing of Printing as a Business (p. 19) After 1480, specialization began: type-founding, paper supply, and printing separated. Anton Koberger of Nuremberg became the first wholesale printer/publisher. The chapter explains the exchange economy — books bartered sheet-for-sheet — and the improvements in roads and carts that widened distribution. Koberger’s Nuremberg Chronicle (1493) was the most successful single edition of the century: ~1,000+ copies, 645 woodblocks yielding 1,809 impressions, sold across Europe (over 500 copies went to Italy alone, with unsold stock in Paris, Strassburg, Prague, Milan, and more). The chapter ends with a striking passage on the economic transformation of the Rhine Valley — rising prosperity, more holidays, more schooling, more book-buying — arguing that the surge in demand, which scribes couldn’t meet, drove the invention of printing and led to the Protestant Reformation.

Chapter 5 — The Printers Encounter the Renaissance (p. 24) German printers crossed the Alps to Italy, starting at Subiaco (1465): Sweynheym and Pannartz printed Cicero, Lactantius, and Augustine in a transitional Gothic-roman type later copied for the Ashendene Press. They moved to Rome (1467), developing the first true roman type. But the Renaissance market was narrow — scholars and aristocrats bought classics but the broader public didn’t — driving many early Italian printers to bankruptcy. Survivors shifted to practical books: military treatises, mathematics, astrology.

Chapter 6 — Venice Becomes the Headquarters of the Book Trade (p. 28) John of Speier brought printing to Venice (1469) with a five-year monopoly, but after his death the city senate refused to extend it, unleashing rapid growth. Nicolaus Jenson, a Frenchman, designed the most admired roman type of the era (praised by modern private presses like Doves Press). Venice’s advantages: cheap paper from northern Italian mills, the world’s best shipping network (books shipped unbound in casks), and the prestige of Italian humanism. By 1480 Venice was the largest book-making centre, with wholesalers (like Octavianus Scotus) commissioning editions and multiple partners sharing risk. Erhard Ratdolt introduced decorative woodcut borders and the modern title-page (1476), specializing in astronomical and geographical works. Ratdolt later returned to Augsburg.

Chapter 7 — Printing Reaches the Whole European World (p. 35) From Mainz, printers went up the Rhine to Basle (a distribution hub for northern France), then to Lyons (the great trading centre for peddlers, specializing in popular vernacular books: romances, household manuals, the Romance of the Rose). A few crossed the Pyrenees into Spain, where Hebrew printers produced masterpieces. The chapter follows printing across the Atlantic: Juan Pablos set up the first American press in Mexico City (1539), printing proclamations, missionary tracts, and the news of the Guatemala earthquake (1540). Antonio Ricardo took printing to Lima, Peru (1584).

Chapter 8 — The First Private Presses (p. 39) Unlike commercial ventures, the first Paris press (1470) was a private Sorbonne establishment — the rector and librarian hired three workmen from Basle to print student texts. The most notable product: Cardinal Bessarion’s Orations for peace against the Turks, sent with printed personal letters to every European ruler. The press lasted three years. The chapter’s centrepiece is William Caxton at Bruges and Westminster: a retired English wool merchant who learned printing at Cologne (1471), set up a press in Bruges to print his own translations (first English book: Recuyell of the Troye Book, c. 1475), then moved to Westminster (1476). He printed over 100 works — Chaucer, Malory, Gower, Aesop, Reynard the Fox — mostly popular English pamphlets now known from single copies, because they were read to destruction. His Fleming foreman, Wynkyn de Worde, inherited the business (1491–1534).

Chapter 9 — The Use of Pictures (p. 44) Woodcuts predate typography: playing cards from Asia, pilgrim saints’ pictures (heiligen) from the Rhine towns, the 1423 St. Christopher and the 1418 Virgin from Malines (Flanders). These evolved into Block Books (Biblia Pauperum, Ars Moriendi, Apocalypse), printed from single engraved blocks. Albrecht Pfister at Bamberg produced the first books combining typography and woodcut illustrations (1461–62). After 1470, illustrated books became general — Augsburg led with vernacular Bibles, Ulm produced Ptolemy’s Cosmographia with maps (1482), and dozens of town chronicles reflected awakening historical consciousness. The Fasciculus Temporum (Rolewinck of Cologne) had 33 Latin/vernacular editions between 1474–1500.

Chapter 10 — The Decade of Picture Books (p. 49) A sudden boom in illustrated popular books during the 1490s in four centres:

  • Basle: Sebastian Brant’s Ship of Fools (1494), 115 illustrations, 25 editions in 15 years
  • Venice: Column-width woodcuts in the Malermi Bible (1490), Dante editions, the Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (Aldus, 1499) — 168 illustrations, unexcelled artistic quality
  • Florence: Cheap illustrated Rappresentazione (Biblical playlets) and Savonarola’s reformist tracts; early experiments with copper engraving (Dante 1481) failed due to printing difficulties
  • Paris: The Book of Hours (Horae) — 170 recorded Paris diocesan editions between 1490–1500 alone, employing hundreds of Flemish and French artists. Pigouchet, Vostre, Kerver, and Verard competed with lavishly bordered editions. The fashion collapsed around 1525 under its own overproduction.

Chapter 11 — The Learned Printers (p. 60) Aldus Manutius at Venice (from 1488) and the Estienne family at Paris. Aldus, supported by the Carpi family, designed compact cursive Greek type (standard ever since), printed Aristotle in 5 folio volumes (1495–98), then launched his revolutionary octavo Latin classics (1500–1501) — the “Everyman’s Library” of its day. The Aldine italic type (modelled on Petrarch’s handwriting), the anchor-and-dolphin mark, and the octavo format made scholarship affordable. Pirated editions plagued him. After his death (1515), his son Paul Manutius and grandson Aldus II struggled to maintain the press as Venice declined. Aldus II died in 1597 — “the most learned man of his time, he lived too late; learning had become erudition, and the Renaissance had given way to the modern world.”

Chapter 12 — The Patrons of Printing (p. 69) Jean Grolier, French royal treasurer, was the greatest 16th-century book collector. His library bindings (Grolieresque interlaced ribbons, “J. Grolierii et amicorum”) became the model for fine binding. The chapter also covers bookplates: the earliest known (Hans Igler, pre-typographic), Hildebrande Brandenburg’s angel-and-shield plate for Buxheim Carthusians, and Dürer’s bookplates for Hieronymous Ebner (1516) and Bilibald Pirckheimer (1524).

Chapter 13 — Conflicting Tendencies at Paris and Geneva (p. 72) The Estienne family’s three generations at Paris: Henri Estienne (from 1501), his son Robert (scholar-printer who compiled the Thesaurus Linguae Latinae at age 30, 1532), and grandson Henri II (Greek Thesaurus, 1572). Robert Estienne’s revision of the New Testament (1528) provoked the Sorbonne — the ensuing controversy led Francis I to ban all printing (1534), but the Parlement refused to register the decree, winning an early victory for freedom of the press. Robert fled to Geneva (Calvinist) after Francis’s death, struggled financially. His son Henri II subsidized by the Fuggers, his Greek Thesaurus ruined by his assistant Scapula’s unauthorized abridgement; Henri died alone in Lyons hospital (1598). His daughter married Isaac Casaubon, carrying the Estienne scholarly inheritance to England.

Chapter 14 — The End of the Manuscript Tradition (p. 77) Simon de Colines (third husband of Henri Estienne’s widow) developed the Paris press into a rival of Aldus’s Academy, issuing carefully designed book series. He employed Geofroy Tory, the greatest typographic decorator — Tory designed title-borders, initials, and printer’s marks, reformed French spelling (introduced Ç), and replaced black-letter with roman type. The French printers of Paris (Vascosan, Granjon, Morel) and Lyons (de Tournes, Paradin) produced books that were “distinctly modern… owing nothing to mediaeval traditions.” This chapter bridges to the final chapter on Antwerp.

Chapter 15 — The End of the Era of the Master Printers (p. 81) Christophe Plantin. Antwerp was supplanting Venice as the world’s commercial metropolis before 1550, with printers controlling a quarter of the town. Unlike Venice’s scholarly Latin books, Antwerp produced entertaining vernacular works in every European language, in huge quantities. Plantin (a French bookbinder arrived 1549) won the commission for Charles V’s funeral volume (1559), then conceived the Polyglott Bible (1568–1572) — 8 volumes, 4 languages, 1,200 paper + 13 vellum copies, 40 workmen, 4 presses, subsidized by Philip II (but chronically underfunded). Plantin died 1589; the business passed to son-in-law Jean Moretus, specializing in Spanish service book monopolies, until the city of Antwerp bought it as the Plantin-Moretus Museum in 1876.

Key Excerpts

Preface (pp. v–viii)

The study of the early history of printing has entered upon a new phase since the World War. It is a double phase, divided by the year 1500. The accumulated information of all that happened before that date is being coordinated, but the results must be held as tentative until the completion of a new catalogue of fifteenth-century books. For the sixteenth century, a beginning has been made, but nothing more, toward bringing together widely scattered data.

A hundred years ago, Ludwig Hain made it possible to study fifteenth-century books intelligently, by printing descriptions of 16,300 titles known to him. Half a century later, Henry Bradshaw showed that the proper way to classify these titles, in order to get at the hidden story of printing, is to group the books from each press in their chronological order. Robert Proctor, in the eighteen-nineties, applied this method to the “Fifteeners” in the British Museum and Bodleian libraries, locating with brilliant intuition nearly half of the total number that were published without any name of place or of printer, or date. Proctor’s work revealed for the first time the details of how printing spread over Europe. It made possible the systematic classification of existing data, and it stimulated the search for documentary material by which to test the inferences derived from the internal evidence of the books themselves.

This outline aims to be a statement of facts which have in most cases long been established, with as much of the newer conclusions drawn from these facts as seems to the author reasonably certain of eventual acceptance. Controversial details have usually been ignored, because any discussion of pros and cons, or even an intelligible statement of the points at issue, would destroy the proportion of the narrative by giving these subjects more space than they deserve.

Alfred W. Pollard, long of the British Museum, is responsible for most of the statements in this outline, particularly for the method by which I reached conclusions in a few cases radically different from his. I have, with his permission, repeated in a few cases his phrasing of certain statements, without quotation marks, because I cannot express these things as accurately and convincingly as he has done.

Chapter 1: The Invention of Typography 1440–1456 (pp. 3–8)

John Gutenberg invented printing before the middle of the fifteenth century. Typography is a more correct term, for what he did was to construct the apparatus for making movable metal letters or type and for using these to produce many copies, all alike, of a book.

Gutenberg was a native of Mainz, whose youthful activity in local politics led to his retirement to Strassburg, where his experimental work on the invention was done. There, according to the testimony of witnesses in a lawsuit in 1439, Gutenberg produced something, the precise character of which he kept secret, but which he was able to sell. Ten years later a Mainz banker, Johann Fust, became interested in the invention and provided money to pay for the paper, wages, and other things required to produce copies of a large Bible.

This is known as the GUTENBERG, the MAZARIN, or the FORTY-TWO-LINE BIBLE… It consists of 641 leaves (or twice that number of pages), 16 by 12 inches, bound in two volumes. Forty-one copies, more or less complete, are still to be seen… Eight of these are now in American public or private libraries.

Peter Schoeffer… may have been employed by Gutenberg, perhaps as the foreman of the shop, while the Bible was printing… This involved the management of six presses, of which two or three were in use for nearly the full period from 1450 to 1456, each with its crew of two or three workmen besides the compositor who set the type for the page which was to be printed next. In a well-organized shop there would also be type-casters to maintain a supply of letters, and workmen to look after a regular supply of blank paper and the proper drying and storage of the printed sheets. Someone, possibly a local ecclesiastic, must have made certain that the text was correct after it was set in type. Presumably a bindery was added to the other activities after the first half of the book was finished.

The only book, except the forty-two-line Bible, which can be connected with Gutenberg is the CATHOLICON, an encyclopaedic dictionary compiled in the thirteenth century by Joannes Balbus, of 373 leaves as large as the Bible. At the end is the colophon… declaring that it was produced at Mainz in 1460 “by the help of the Most High… who oftentimes reveals to the lowly that which he hides from the wise.”

The Aldine Press — Venice (pp. 60–66)

Aldus Manutius… determined to produce the Greek classics in a convenient form, at a moderate price, and in a continuous series. The enterprise was successfully launched in 1495, when he issued five volumes of the works of Aristotle in a folio edition. Others followed, and he was able to publish the whole of Aristotle in five folio volumes by 1498. This did not satisfy his ambition. He planned a library of all the great authors in handy size, carefully edited and sold at a price which would bring them within the reach of students and book-lovers of moderate means.

The type which made the octavo editions possible was an adaptation of the idea which had already made his Greek books a success. It was modelled on the cursive handwriting in current use… This type was at first called “Venetian,” or “Italic” in other countries, and the latter name has clung to it.

In the summer of 1502 Aldus adopted, as a mark by which his editions might be recognized, the anchor and dolphin, the first signifying surety and the other speed. This mark, as well as the Aldine name, won a contemporary fame which has never lessened during later centuries.

(The book also notes Erasmus’s Adagia were printed by Aldus in 1508 — 276 leaves — line 2114.)

The Estienne Family — Paris and Geneva (pp. 72–76)

Robert Estienne was a good printer, and a scholar of distinction who took an important part in bringing the Renaissance to France. He prepared “Thesaurus linguae Latinae” (1532) when he was thirty years old…

Robert Estienne began his career by revising the text of the New Testament… Violent protests were at once voiced at the Sorbonne against the audacity of Robert Estienne… When the controversy became more annoying, in 1534, Francis I issued a peremptory order forbidding all printing. This produced another unexpected trouble, for the Parliament refused to register the decree. A revised order, forbidding the printing of new books, met the same refusal, and when the King accepted the situation, the first move had been won in the struggle for the FREEDOM OF THE PRESS.

The End of the Manuscript Tradition — Geofroy Tory (pp. 77–80)

Simon de Colines’s greatest contribution to the fame of the establishment came when he recognized the genius of GEOFROY TORY and employed him to design decorations for its books… He brought about the abandonment of the traditional black-letter type in favor of the modern roman letter; he introduced the Ç and gave a tremendous impetus to self-consciousness among the French printers, leading them to consider their responsibility for the contents as well as the appearance of the books on which they put their names.

The Antwerp / Plantin Chapter — Full Text (pp. 81–86)

This is the most directly relevant portion for the Dutch Republic knowledge base. It is the final chapter of the book, “The End of the Era of the Master Printers.”

The Era of the Master Printers ended with CHRISTOPHE PLANTIN. Since his time there have been many great masters of printing establishments who were practical craftsmen, some of whom, like the two Charles Whittinghams, have profoundly influenced the development of book-making. Before Plantin, from the days of Koberger and of the early Venetians, there had been owners of printing plants whose business ability made them sellers rather than makers of books. But it was the Master, exercising a direct oversight of the activities of compositors, pressmen, and binders, who gave to the industry its characteristic features down to the later decades of the sixteenth century. After 1600 the publisher dominated the craftsman.

ANTWERP, the capital of the Spanish Netherlands, was supplanting Venice as the world’s shipping metropolis before 1550. The printers already controlled one quarter of the town, and had begun to rival the Italians as purveyors of books, without taking anything away from the older city. The social and economic changes of half a century had doomed the books — treatises on the Canon Law, the Church Fathers, mediaeval medical works, editions of the ancient Classics made authoritative by the names of famous scholars — which were still the backbone of the Venetian book trade. Instead of these books, which appealed to those who longed for culture and learning, hundreds of Antwerp workmen were prosperously employed in making books which were intended to entertain the readers. They were printed in every European language, in quantities as much larger than the editions of those other more respectable works as they are now more difficult to find. Rarely does the hunter through the old bookshops come upon an Antwerp sixteenth-century imprint; and when he does, he may not recognize it, for the title-page is apt to say that it was printed at Paris or Philopolis or some equally improbable locality.

Christophe Plantin was a young French bookbinder seeking his fortune when he went to Antwerp in 1549. He made his way to recognition by the court officials, until an injury forced him to abandon his trade and take up the lighter physical labor of printing. By 1559 he had won a position which gave him the commission for printing the monumental volume containing the official record of the ceremonies attending the funeral of Charles the Fifth. This led to other similar tasks executed for the government, which gave him contemporary fame, and eventual bankruptcy. He built up a sound business, and prospered whenever he was free to attend to this. But like many another, the fascination of doing a grand book was more than he could resist, and then as always it was impossible to do the monumental volume, to his own critical satisfaction, without exceeding the amount which seems proper to a layman unfamiliar with the time and attention imperatively demanded by a piece of fine printing.

Plantin’s grandest idea became a reality through the assistance of Spanish ecclesiastical and court officials. Fifty years before, another Spaniard, Cardinal Ximenes, had fostered the first Polyglott Bible. This had long been unprocurable by scholars, and Plantin proposed to issue a more adequate work which should include the texts of the Holy Scriptures in all the languages from which they are derived. Philip II became interested in the idea and promised to subsidize it. The royal promises were ample to justify the venture; but the royal treasurer had many other claims upon the funds in hand, and with the best of intentions, Philip was unable to do all that he meant to.

Plantin began by training a special staff of workmen. A Hebrew Grammar was printed to familiarize them with this language, and when he found a satisfactory proof-reader, he married him to a daughter, that there might be less danger of losing him. New fonts of type were ordered from Robert Granjon of Lyons and Claude Garamond of Paris. The initial order for paper called for 3000 reams, and 400 dozen skins were ordered for the vellum copies.

Sample pages of the Polyglott, with the text in four languages, were shown at the Franckfurt book fair in 1566. There was talk of a rival work under Lutheran auspices, with the Elector of Saxony as patron, but it was obvious that the effort to print the two would be suicidal for both. It was this danger — that the undertaking might be contaminated by Protestant influence — which helped to convince Philip II that it must be done under Spanish control. By midsummer of 1568 the specimen sheets had received final approval, and a few months later the work started. Forty workmen, with two, and later four, presses, were kept steadily employed for three years. The edition was 1200 copies, on paper of four different sizes, and thirteen copies on vellum for the King. The number of volumes in which it was bound varies from six on the ordinary paper to eleven for those on vellum. It was completed in 1572.

Plantin’s Polyglott Bible is still unrivalled as a printer’s achievement. But the printer had to pay his workmen in order that the work might not stop, and when the remittances from the Spanish treasury did not arrive, he had to turn to a public-spirited Jewish money-lender, Louis Perez, who must have known how precarious was the security. The King endeavored to help the situation, and made it worse by ordering a series of large service books which he thought of presenting to each church in the kingdom. This did not please the printers of Spain, who made their influence felt, and the plan was given up after Plantin had committed himself to further heavy expenditures. A more tangible reward was a royal grant giving him a monopoly for printing the authorized service books for the Spanish domains.

Plantin died in 1589, and the business passed to a son-in-law, JEAN MORETUS, who had advanced money to keep it going. Abandoning all other lines, Moretus and his successors devoted their attention to the monopoly of service books. The mark of the compass was no longer found on handsome books, but the profits of the establishment steadily accumulated. The business remained in the family and was carried on in the original Plantin house until 1876, when the city of Antwerp and the Belgium government united to purchase it as a public museum. Plantin was a sound business man in principle if not always in practice, keeping all of his account books carefully and preserving his correspondence. These documentary records supplement the actual presses, fonts of type, and cases filled with woodblocks and metal cuts, with the desks of the proof-readers and the incidental paraphernalia of a sixteenth-century printing office, all together making this museum the most significant and instructive of printing meccas.

Relevance to the Dutch Republic Knowledge Base

This source is directly relevant to several existing and potential concept pages:

  1. Antwerp’s commercial supremacy — Antwerp was “supplanting Venice as the world’s shipping metropolis before 1550,” with printers controlling a quarter of the town. This feeds into the broader economic story of the Dutch Republic’s Golden Age.

  2. The shift from scholarly to popular publishing — Antwerp printers produced books “intended to entertain the readers… printed in every European language” at much larger quantities than traditional Latin scholarly works. This is a key data point for Dutch knowledge culture and the commercialization of print.

  3. Christophe Plantin and the Polyglott Bible — A monumental 8-volume work (1568–1572) with 40 workmen, 4 presses, 1,200 copies, financed by Philip II but chronically underfunded. Ties together Spanish imperial patronage, Antwerp commerce, and the Reformation-era religious publishing wars.

  4. The Plantin-Moretus Museum — The business stayed in the family until 1876, preserving 16th-century presses, type, account books, and correspondence — an unparalleled resource for understanding early modern printing economics.

  5. Bibliographical methodology — The preface (Winship/Pollard/Bradshaw/Proctor) describes the development of the analytical bibliography used to trace the spread of printing across Europe, the same methodology that underpins modern book history.

  6. The wider European context — The book traces printing’s spread from Mainz → Germany → Italy (Venice) → France → Low Countries → Spain → England. It provides context for how ideas, texts, and technical knowledge flowed through Europe into the Dutch Republic.

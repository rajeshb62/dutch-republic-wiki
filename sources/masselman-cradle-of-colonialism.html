George Masselman, The Cradle of Colonialism. Yale University Press, 1963. The extracted raw text has 23,836 lines and about 1.2 MB of readable OCR from a scanned copy. It is the wiki’s strongest narrative source for Dutch corporate colonialism in Southeast Asia, centered on the VOC, jan-pieterszoon-coen, the spice monopoly, batavia, the english-east-india-company, john-jourdain, bantam, Ambon, and Banda. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

Contents at a Glance

Masselman presents Dutch colonialism as a modern corporate form of empire rooted in capital investment, private enterprise, monopoly rights, and the East Indies spice trade. The preface argues that the Dutch, rather than the Portuguese, Spanish, or English, first converted overseas capital investment into a durable colonial-commercial system in the East Indies; English failure in East Asia redirected English attention toward India. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

The book has three large movements. Book I explains the Dutch background: delta geography, sea labor, herring, Baltic and Iberian trade, Amsterdam, the Revolt, capital formation, merchant organization, Linschoten, Plancius, Arctic attempts, and the first Cape voyages. Book II follows the pre-company rush to Asia, the political creation of the VOC, early operational failures, Portuguese/Asian trade background, and the company’s first experiments with armed monopoly. Book III becomes a long Coen-centered narrative: biography, English rivalry, Coen’s imperial program, Jacatra/Batavia, Banda, English withdrawal, free-trade disputes, and the post-Coen consequences of monopoly colonialism. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

Book I — The Background

  • Ch. 1: The Land and the People — Opens from the Dutch physical environment: peat-digging, lakes, erosion, the “water-wolf,” salt/fuel problems, drainage, dikes, and the constant double struggle against water and land loss. Masselman uses geography as causal background, not scenery: scarcity of land and fuel, vulnerability to flooding, and the discipline of water management help explain why the Dutch became technically practical, commercially restless, and maritime. Mine this chapter for polder-model-water-management, agriculture, and the material foundations of dutch-republic. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 2: The Sea — Moves from the land to the sea economy, especially fishing, herring regulation, curing, packing, town marks, crews, and the institutional supervision of quality. The chapter treats maritime labor as a school for seamanship and as a commercial system linking North Sea fishing, ship handling, provisioning, salt demand, and export discipline. Useful for connecting naval-warfare-and-sea-power with industry-and-manufacturing and with the later ability to staff long-distance fleets. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 3: The Sea Lanes Become Longer — Traces the northward movement of European trade centers from Bruges to Antwerp and then toward Amsterdam, emphasizing silting waterways, Scheldt access, wool, the Hansa, Iberian connections, and the broadening range of Dutch shipping. The chapter is about how Dutch commerce outgrew local waters before the Indies: Baltic carriage, Iberian salt and wine, Antwerp’s rise and fall, and the geographic logic that made Holland and Zeeland middlemen between northern and southern Europe. Mine for amsterdam, antwerp, urbanization, and the pre-Asian roots of Dutch carrying trade. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 4: The Town — Focuses on Amsterdam as civic-commercial machine: protected harbor on the IJ, access through the Zuiderzee, inland connections, municipal development, trade privileges, urban government, migration, religious change, and the 1578 Alteration. Masselman describes the town as a platform where merchants, magistrates, refugees, Calvinists, and trade infrastructure combined; Amsterdam becomes not simply a port but the institutional environment from which overseas ventures could be financed and organized. Use for amsterdam, dutch-republic-power-structure, public-finance, and urbanization. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 5: Independence at Any Price — Places overseas expansion inside the Revolt: Spanish pressure, Parma, Leicester, Calvinist agitation, merchant-magistrates, war finance, and the conflict between religious zeal and commercial survival. The chapter shows the Dutch fighting for independence while still needing trade with enemy-linked markets, creating a persistent tension between God, country, and profit. It is useful background for religion-and-politics, william-of-orange, philip-ii-of-spain, johan-van-oldenbarnevelt, and the later logic of state-backed commerce. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 6: Factors of Trade Expansion — Explains why Dutch trade expanded during war instead of collapsing: Mediterranean ventures, herring growth, salt shortages, Cape Verde salt, Guinea, African coasts, shipbuilding, low freight rates, insurance, credit, and the willingness to enter risky markets. Masselman emphasizes practical pressures—salt, fish, shipping, routes, capital, and enemy exclusions—as forces pushing the Dutch from European trade into Atlantic and Asian opportunity. Mine for public-finance, naval-warfare-and-sea-power, amsterdam-exchange-bank, and the economic preconditions of voc-finance-and-capital. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 7: From Guilds to Capitalism — Presents the organizational transition from medieval guild restraint to joint ventures, outside investment, limited risk, divided shares, and early stock-company forms. The chapter is one of the best places in the source for Masselman’s capitalism thesis: the Indies trade required more capital than single merchants could risk, and Dutch practice made pooled investment normal before the VOC made it permanent. Use for voc-finance-and-capital, voc-governance, public-finance, and the broader “cradle of colonialism” argument. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 8: Three Merchants and a Minister — Introduces the Amsterdam men and networks behind the Asian turn: Hendrik Hudde, Reynier Pauw, Balthasar de Moucheron, Petrus Plancius, and the commercial-religious refugee world that carried intelligence, capital, and anti-Iberian zeal. The chapter links merchants, Calvinist exile, navigation, map knowledge, and Amsterdam politics; it is a bridge between domestic urban history and concrete preparations for the Indies. Mine for petrus-plancius, amsterdam, dutch-cartography-and-atlases, and possibly a future Reynier Pauw page. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 9: Linschoten and His Itinerary — Gives the route-intelligence chapter: jan-huygen-van-linschoten, his Portuguese service, information on Goa, Madagascar, the Indian Ocean, Portuguese weakness, sailing directions, Japan, China, and how the Itinerario broke the Iberian information monopoly. Masselman treats Linschoten as a converter of secret imperial experience into publishable Dutch commercial knowledge. Use this chapter heavily for pre-company-expeditions, portuguese-in-asia, dutch-cartography-and-atlases, and dutch-knowledge-culture. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 10: North to the Indies — Covers the first serious Northeast Passage planning: Plancius, Moucheron, Barentsz, Oldenbarnevelt, the States of Holland and Zeeland, the Kara Sea, Novaya Zemlya, and the assumption that a northern route to Cathay/Cipango might bypass Iberian power. The chapter is as much about political-commercial coordination as about exploration; it shows provincial bargaining and merchant ambition shaping geographic experiment. Use for arctic-exploration, petrus-plancius, johan-van-oldenbarnevelt, and Dutch route strategy before the Cape route won. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 11: South to the Indies — Narrates the first Dutch Cape-route voyage: Houtman, Plancius’s instructions, ship fitting, councils of navigators and merchants, Cape/Madagascar route problems, Bantam, pepper, internal quarrels, violence, limited profit, and proof of possibility. Masselman makes this a messy experimental voyage rather than a heroic breakthrough: navigational knowledge was incomplete, command was divided, and the return mattered because it proved Dutch ships could reach Asian markets. Mine for houtman-brothers, pre-company-expeditions, bantam, spice-trade, and early company governance problems. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 12: The Second and Third Voyages North — Returns to Arctic attempts after the first southern voyage, covering state support, Hakluyt’s manuscript, Moucheron, the States-General, Barentsz, Novaya Zemlya, and the slow death of the northern-route dream. The chapter is useful because it shows that the Cape route did not immediately eliminate northern hopes; Dutch expansion developed through competing route projects before commercial results selected the southern passage. Use for arctic-exploration, pre-company-expeditions, and the interaction of English geographic intelligence with Dutch planning. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

Book II — The East India Company

  • Ch. 1: Amsterdam Collects — Follows the second southern voyage and the gathering of knowledge from Houtman’s failures: better routing around the Cape, Plancius’s advice, stronger leadership, Van Neck, Heemskerck, Bantam, Ambon, Ternate, Banda, and Portuguese weakness. The chapter shows Amsterdam learning from error and converting a disappointing first voyage into a more profitable, systematic venture. Mine for pre-company-expeditions, bantam, spice-trade, portuguese-in-asia, and Amsterdam’s role as organizer. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 2: The Rush to the Indies — Describes the explosion of competing voyages in 1598–1602: Amsterdam, Zeeland, Moucheron, Van Neck, Heemskerck, English intelligence through John Davis, Bantam pepper shortages, overbuying, inter-Dutch rivalry, and the beginning of market self-damage. This is the key chapter for why consolidation became necessary: too many Dutch companies chased the same spices, bid up Asian prices, and risked dumping returns in Europe. Use for pre-company-expeditions, english-east-india-company, bantam, and voc-finance-and-capital. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 3: The Prime Mover of the United Company — Narrows to political pressure for merger, especially Oldenbarnevelt and the States’ interest in preventing Dutch competitors from ruining each other while war with Spain continued. The chapter also includes the capture of the Portuguese carack and the emergence of kraak porcelain as proof that armed seizure and Asian luxury trade could produce spectacular returns. Use for johan-van-oldenbarnevelt, voc-governance, dutch-republic-power-structure, china-trade, and the political origin of VOC unity. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 4: The Formation of the United East India Company — Gives the detailed institutional story of the 1602 VOC charter: Amsterdam versus Zeeland, Oldenbarnevelt’s mediation, chambers, directors, the Seventeen, monopoly rights, state powers, capital commitments, duration, and the balance between provincial jealousy and unified policy. This is the source’s central chapter for VOC structure. Mine it for voc-dutch-east-india-company, voc-governance, voc-finance-and-capital, amsterdam, and the States-General’s delegation of warlike powers to a company. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 5: By Trial and Error — Shows the early VOC discovering that monopoly on paper did not solve Asian realities: Portuguese resistance, English rivalry, Ceylon/Kandy, Achin, Ambon, Banda, factories, instructions, uncertain alliances, and the practical problem of enforcing purchases over enormous distances. The chapter is useful for the gap between corporate design in the Netherlands and operational improvisation in Asia. Use for portuguese-in-asia, voc-asian-headquarters, malacca-and-malay-world, spice-trade, and early factory policy. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 6: War and Trade Do Not Mix — Develops the title’s problem through Van der Hagen, Matelief, Goa, Calicut, Ambon, Banda, Bantam, Portuguese fleets, and the costs of using armed expeditions to secure trade. Masselman shows that warfare might open ports or damage Portuguese power, but it also consumed capital, delayed returns, confused commercial priorities, and exposed the company to local-political complexity. Mine for voc-governance, naval-warfare-and-sea-power, portuguese-in-asia, banda-extirpation, and the trade/war tension that later Coen tried to resolve by territorial command. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 7: The Seventeen Under Attack — Covers the VOC’s first domestic crisis: falling share prices, pepper gluts, failed attacks on Portuguese strongholds, criticism of the Seventeen, Isaac Le Maire, Lyntgens, political complaints, and shareholder pressure. The chapter is excellent for showing that the VOC was contested from the start, not an immediately successful machine. Use for voc-governance, voc-finance-and-capital, amsterdam, and the early Dutch securities-market story. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 8: 1607: The Burgeoning Year — Treats 1607 as a turning point across several fronts: Dutch religious refuge for English separatists, Oldenbarnevelt, Spanish negotiations, Heemskerck and Gibraltar, Matelief, Ternate, Asian expansion, and the broader confidence of the Republic. In VOC terms, the chapter marks movement from defensive, uncertain ventures toward more assertive sea power and Asian treaty-making. Use for voc-1607-turning-point, naval-warfare-and-sea-power, religion-and-politics, and portuguese-in-asia. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 9: Early Asiatic Trade: the Hindu Period — Provides deep Asian background rather than Dutch narrative: Van Leur’s trade model, commenda-style finance, monsoon shipping, small cargoes, active traveling merchants, passive investors, Hindu-Buddhist polities, Vijaya, Kertanagara, Chinese contact, and pre-European Southeast Asian trade. This is one of the best chapters for preventing a Eurocentric reading of the VOC: Masselman sketches the commercial world Europeans entered rather than created. Use for asian-trade-before-europeans, india-textile-trade, malacca-and-malay-world, and java-and-mataram. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 10: Background in Asia — Continues Asian context through Islam, Chinese and Japanese trade, Turkish/Ottoman pressure on older routes, Venice/Genoa, Malacca, Java, Sumatra, the spice islands, and the political-commercial environment of Southeast Asia before Dutch dominance. The chapter explains why Europeans came by sea and why control over choke points, ports, rulers, and monsoon routes mattered. Mine for malacca-and-malay-world, java-and-mataram, china-trade, japan-trade, spice-trade, and asian-trade-before-europeans. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 11: The Portuguese Age — Summarizes the Estado da India: Vasco da Gama’s arrival, Calicut and the Samorin, Muslim merchant opposition, fortresses, naval violence, Goa, Malacca, Hormuz, Macao, Portuguese administrative weakness, corruption, crusading claims, and limits of trade organization. Masselman uses the Portuguese as both precedent and contrast: they built armed positions in Asia, but not the Dutch form of capitalized corporate monopoly. Use for portuguese-in-asia, malacca-and-malay-world, china-trade, and VOC displacement of Iberian networks. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

Book III — Jan Pieterszoon Coen

  • Ch. 1: Jan Pieterszoon Coen — Begins Coen’s biography: baptism in Hoorn, family background, father Pieter Willemszoon van Twisk, Hoorn’s commercial world, the adoption/use of the Coen name, early schooling, the town’s maritime-commercial environment, and later Italian training. The chapter is useful for seeing Coen as product of Dutch provincial merchant culture rather than as an isolated imperial genius. Mine for jan-pieterszoon-coen, Hoorn context, and the personnel background of voc-governance. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 2: The Bottom of the Ladder — Follows Coen’s entry into VOC service at low rank, fleet mustering, recruitment, sailors, soldiers, low wages, harsh shipboard conditions, mixed crews, and the path by which ambitious young men could rise inside the company. Masselman uses the chapter to describe the labor ecology of the VOC: skilled officers, underpaid servants, waterfront recruits, soldiers, discipline, disease, and mortality. Use for voc-shipboard-life, voc-governance, voc-dutch-east-india-company, and Coen’s early institutional formation. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 3: Coen’s First Contact with the Indies — Covers Coen’s first Asian experience under Verhoef: Portuguese encounters, Calicut, Banda, Bandanese negotiations, Dutch violence and vulnerability, English presence, Bantam, and the lessons Coen drew from fragmented trade, weak authority, and treacherous alliance politics. This chapter is important because it provides the experiential foundation for Coen’s later insistence on centralized command and coercive monopoly. Mine for jan-pieterszoon-coen, banda-extirpation, bantam, portuguese-in-asia, and voc-monopoly-system. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 4: The English versus the Dutch — Steps back to compare English and Dutch economic development before the EIC/VOC clash: wool, staples, Bruges, cloth, monarchy, London, English frustration, Drake, royal support, and the relative Dutch advantage in European carrying trade. Masselman is setting up the rivalry by explaining why England felt both entitled and late in Asian commerce. Use for england, london, english-east-india-company, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and Dutch-English structural comparison within existing concept pages. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 5: The English East India Company — Gives the EIC’s early institutional and financial context: Elizabethan chartering, London merchants, undercapitalization, intermittent voyages, English pride, weak sustained support, and the contrast with Dutch scale. The chapter is especially useful for why the English could challenge but not displace the VOC in the spice islands. Mine for english-east-india-company, london, england, spice-trade, and the English side of anglo-dutch-rivalry. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 6: A Servant of the English Company — Biographical chapter on john-jourdain: family and will details, Old Style/Gregorian date issues, his voyage from Woolwich, English company service, Ascension, Middleton, Hawkins, Portuguese opposition, factory life, quarrels among English servants, and Jourdain’s experience before becoming Coen’s counterpart. This is the strongest single source section for a full Jourdain page. Mine it for john-jourdain, english-east-india-company, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and the personnel culture of English Asian trade. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 7: Up the Ladder — Brings Coen and Jourdain into direct rivalry as Coen rises: Coen’s return to Holland, promotion to chief merchant, reports to the Seventeen, Bantam, Ambon, Luhu, Kambela, clove competition, English-Dutch price escalation, and Coen’s growing authority. The chapter shows Coen moving from talented servant to strategist and Jourdain emerging as the English obstacle. Use for jan-pieterszoon-coen, john-jourdain, bantam, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and amboyna-massacre background. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 8: Blueprint for Empire — Centers on Coen’s programmatic vision: Dutch colonies, offensive strategy, planted settlers, strong fleets, control of Asian trade, use of Portuguese colonial precedent, and the argument that trade could not be secured by scattered factories alone. This is the key chapter for Coen’s imperial ideology and for the transition from company trade to company empire. Mine for voc-asian-headquarters, voc-monopoly-system, colonial-empire, jan-pieterszoon-coen, and the territorial logic behind batavia. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 9: The Rendezvous — Explains the practical problem Coen wanted to solve: the need for a fixed Asian rendezvous and command center. It covers Bantam, Djakarta/Jacatra, Mataram, Gerard Reynst, inter-Asian capital needs, return-cargo financing, local rulers, and the limits of running the Indies from dispersed factories. This is a core chapter for voc-asian-headquarters, batavia, bantam, java-and-mataram, and the financial geography of the VOC in Asia. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 10: The Price of Monopoly — Shows monopoly becoming coercive policy: decrees against native trade, ultimata to coastal princes, Ambon/Banda/Malacca/Solor, English interference, Coen’s contract logic, confiscation, and the claim that monopoly rights justified restricting other Asian traders. The chapter is crucial for the moral and practical cost of VOC spice control: free trade disappears under the needs of exclusive supply. Use for voc-monopoly-system, spice-trade, banda-extirpation, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and colonial-empire. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 11: Second in Command — Covers Coen under Laurens Reael and the internal politics of VOC command: low salaries, temptation of private gain, European family absence, servants, soldiers, discipline, Reael’s more conciliatory policy, Coen’s disagreement, and the problem of enforcing company aims through imperfect personnel. The chapter is valuable for the contrast between Coen’s hard-line monopoly and Reael’s softer approach. Mine for voc-governance, jan-pieterszoon-coen, voc-shipboard-life, and internal VOC administrative culture. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 12: The Final Rung — Narrates Coen’s elevation to Governor-General amid tensions with Reael, the Seventeen, English/French competition, Bantam, and Dutch expectations. It shows why the directors chose Coen despite his abrasive tone: he had clarity, discipline, and a plan for making the VOC’s Asian system profitable and defensible. Use for jan-pieterszoon-coen, voc-governance, bantam, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and the lead-up to the Jacatra crisis. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 13: 1618 — Presents Coen in command and pressing the Seventeen for courage, ships, capital, and scale. The chapter shows him arguing that parsimony ruined great enterprises, sending laden ships home, preparing for confrontation in Java, and seeing himself as executor of a larger design than ordinary trade. Mine for jan-pieterszoon-coen, voc-finance-and-capital, voc-asian-headquarters, and the language of VOC imperial will. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 14: The Crisis — Covers the immediate crisis with Bantam, Djakarta/Jacatra, and the English: Jourdain’s appointment as principal English agent, London capital, English decision to stand firm, Coen’s exposed position, Dale, Widjaja Krama, and the diplomatic-military compression that made Jacatra decisive. This is one of the best chapters for the English-Dutch-Javanese triangle before Batavia. Use for anglo-dutch-rivalry, john-jourdain, english-east-india-company, bantam, batavia, and java-and-mataram. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 15: The Siege — Reconstructs the siege of the Dutch fort at Jacatra through the roles of Djakarta, Bantam, and the English rather than through heroics. Masselman stresses the local ruler’s desire to control his own town, Bantam’s jealousy of Djakarta’s trade, English opportunism, Dutch isolation, internal command problems under Van den Broecke, and the uncertain survival of the VOC position before Coen’s return. Mine for batavia, bantam, java-and-mataram, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and the prehistory of Batavia’s foundation. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 16: The Birth of an Empire — Narrates Coen’s return, storming and destruction of Djakarta, burning of the dalem and mosque, withdrawal of enemies, and the decision to establish the long-desired rendezvous as Batavia. This is where Masselman’s narrative turns from trade crisis to territorial founding: a VOC fort becomes a Dutch city and legal-political base in Java. Use for batavia, jan-pieterszoon-coen, voc-asian-headquarters, bantam, and colonial-empire. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 17: Steps Toward Consolidation — Details the building of Batavia as a VOC jurisdiction: Court of Justice, ordinances, tolls, Chinese taxes, arrack, leaseholds, plots, law and order, penalties, English tolls, and the assertion that the company now held Dutch territory in Asia. It also follows the effect of the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of Defense reaching Asia and complicating Coen’s victory. Mine for batavia, voc-asian-headquarters, voc-governance, china-trade, and Coen’s transformation of company factory into colonial state. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 18: The Conquest of Banda — Covers Coen’s Banda campaign, Bandanese resistance, Dutch military occupation, depopulation, forced control of nutmeg production, and the violent enforcement of exclusive contracts. Masselman treats Banda as the clearest case of monopoly colonialism becoming conquest: the spice islands are reorganized for company supply rather than local trade. Use for banda-extirpation, voc-monopoly-system, spice-trade, jan-pieterszoon-coen, and the darker side of colonial-empire. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 19: The English Withdrawal — Describes the weakening English position after the Treaty of Defense, capital shortage, inability to sustain the required fleet share, pullback from eastern spice competition, and the contrast between London hesitation and VOC infrastructure at Batavia. Coen reads English weakness as vindication of his hard line, while the Seventeen and States-General still have diplomatic constraints. Mine for english-east-india-company, anglo-dutch-rivalry, england, london, batavia, and the path toward English concentration on India. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 20: Monopoly or Free Trade? — Presents the policy dispute after Coen’s first governorship: Coen’s warning not to yield to English or French demands, the Seventeen’s instructions, States-General diplomacy, questions of private/free trade, and the tension between national interest, company rights, and European alliances. This is the main chapter for the ideological conflict between monopoly and open commerce inside Dutch policy. Use for voc-monopoly-system, voc-governance, spice-trade, anglo-dutch-rivalry, and public-finance. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Ch. 21: Mission Accomplished — Concludes Coen’s program with Batavia under threat from Mataram and Sultan Agung, Surabaya and Javanese politics, Batavia’s survival, Coen’s second governorship, and his sense that the essential framework of the VOC Asian empire had been established. The chapter is useful for linking Coen’s maritime-commercial project to inland Javanese power and to the defense of Batavia. Mine for batavia, java-and-mataram, jan-pieterszoon-coen, voc-asian-headquarters, and the early limits of Dutch power on Java. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Epilogue — Pulls the argument together: the Dutch came for spices, began with free enterprise, discovered that long-distance armed trade required heavy capital and coordination, and then created monopoly structures that damaged native trade, producers, company servants, stockholders, and spice-producing societies. Masselman contrasts Dutch corporate colonialism with Spanish/Portuguese conquest and crusade, emphasizing that commercial monopoly produced its own form of empire and impoverishment. Use for colonial-empire, voc-dutch-east-india-company, voc-monopoly-system, spice-trade, and synthesis on the costs of Dutch empire. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

  • Afterword: Three Men — Profiles Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, Hugo Grotius, and Reynier Pauw as the Dutch-side architects and interpreters of the system Coen built in Asia. Oldenbarnevelt appears as political broker of the VOC and later victim of Dutch domestic conflict; Grotius as legal/intellectual defender of Dutch seizure and maritime claims; Pauw as Amsterdam power figure linking municipal interests, commerce, and company politics. Mine for johan-van-oldenbarnevelt, philosophy, amsterdam, voc-governance, and a possible future page on Reynier Pauw or Grotius as VOC jurist. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

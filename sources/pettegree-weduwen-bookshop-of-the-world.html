About the Authors

Andrew Pettegree is Professor of Modern History at the University of St Andrews and one of the world’s leading experts on the history of the book. Arthur der Weduwen is a historian of the Dutch Republic and book culture at the same institution. They are co-directors of the Universal Short Title Catalogue (USTC) project, which underpins the quantitative data in this book.

Scope and Approach

The book opens with a striking contrast: Rembrandt owned only 22 books at his bankruptcy in 1656, yet the Dutch Republic produced an estimated 300 million books and traded at least 4 million at auction. The authors argue that books have been “written out of the narrative of the Dutch Golden Age” — overshadowed by painting — despite being far more pervasive in Dutch life and a major export industry. The approach is quantitative and thematic, drawing on the vast data of the USTC, auction records, probate inventories, and archival sources.

Structure

Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

Prelude — Making Room for Books (p. 1) Sets up the central thesis. Dutch homes had 3 million paintings on their walls but produced 300 million books. The book trade was a major export industry; Dutch printing was admired across Europe. Yet books are largely absent from the standard Golden Age narrative. The Prelude argues that the Dutch book world was both a commercial powerhouse and the foundation of a uniquely informed, politically engaged citizenry. Key early statistics: the Dutch published more books per capita than any other nation; they invented sophisticated marketing (newspaper ads, auctions); and Amsterdam became the world’s book capital.

Chapter 1 — Beginnings (p. 27) Opens with Louis Elzevier arriving footsore in Leiden in 1580 after failed ventures in Liège and Douai. He was still in debt to Plantin, who forced him to sell his house. His salvation: buying books for Leiden professors at the Frankfurt Book Fair, which was then the centre of the European book world (Venice, Paris, and Antwerp publishers dominated). The chapter traces how the Dutch book trade emerged from the Revolt — the closure of the Scheldt (Antwerp’s decline) and the influx of Southern Netherlandish refugees (including printers and booksellers) into the north. By mid-century Amsterdam had replaced Frankfurt as the “bookshop of the world.”

Chapter 2 — A Poisonous Peace (p. 45) Focuses on the pamphlet wars around the Twelve Years’ Truce (1609–1621) and the Oldenbarnevelt crisis (1618–1619). Oldenbarnevelt was attacked by pamphlets accusing him of treason; when he fought back with a printed Remonstrantie, it sold in four editions. The chapter shows how printing became a weapon in political and religious conflict — the Remonstrant/Contra-Remonstrant dispute, the Synod of Dordrecht (1618–1619), and the execution of Oldenbarnevelt all played out in print. The Dutch Republic’s lack of centralised censorship allowed a vibrant, often vicious, pamphlet culture.

Chapter 3 — News Cycles (p. 68) The invention of the newspaper. Broer Jansz, an embedded war correspondent with Maurice of Nassau’s army, rushed from The Hague to Amsterdam to publish a special edition when Oldenbarnevelt was beheaded (1619). Amsterdam had two long-lived newspapers by 1618 (Broer Jansz and Caspar van Hilten); within 25 years it had six, publishing on five days of the week. The chapter covers news gathering, subscription models, government regulation, and how the Dutch pioneered the commercial news market. The couranten (corantos) were single sheets, sold by subscription or at bookshops, and were the foundation of the modern newspaper.

Chapter 4 — To the Ends of the Earth (p. 90) The Dutch book in global trade. Books accompanied VOC ships to the East Indies — devotional works for sailors, Bibles, Calvin’s Institutes, anti-Catholic polemics, and the phenomenally successful Christelijcke Zee-vaert (Christian Sea Voyage), which went through at least ten editions before any single copy survived. Also covers the export of Dutch Bibles and schoolbooks to the Baltic, England, and the New World, and the role of the Dutch in supplying books to Protestant communities across Europe.

Chapter 5 — The Marketplace of Devotion (p. 121) The religious book market — the largest single category of Dutch publishing. The chapter examines how the Reformed Church’s hybrid status (official but not monopolistic) created a market where multiple faiths (Catholic, Mennonite, Lutheran, Remonstrant) competed through print. The Statenbijbel (States Bible, 1637) was the great official project. But alongside it flourished an enormous trade in devotional works, sermons, psalm books, catechisms, and edifying literature. Many of these were cheap, small-format books “read to destruction” — hence the disappearance of entire editions.

Chapter 6 — Schoolmaster Bartjens (p. 148) The educational market. Willem Bartjens’s arithmetic textbook Cijfferinghe (1604) was one of the most successful Dutch books ever — but his son Gerrit’s attempt to control it with a privilege failed, as rival publishers reprinted it with impunity. The chapter covers schoolbooks, ABC books, grammars, and the high literacy rates that made the Dutch Republic unique. Literacy was a practical necessity in a commercial society, and the educational publishing market was correspondingly huge. Also covers the privilégie (privilege/copyright) system and its limitations.

Chapter 7 — The Life Academic (p. 171) Leiden University and academic publishing. Leiden, as the only university in Holland (until Amsterdam’s Athenaeum), was the intellectual powerhouse. The chapter covers the Elzeviers’ transformation from bookbinders to scholarly publishers, the role of Leiden professors as authors and editors, and the importance of the Frankfurt Fair for international distribution. Also covers the founding of Utrecht University (1636) and the Amsterdam Athenaeum Illustre (1632), which expanded the academic market. The Dutch Republic became a haven for exiled scholars and controversial authors (Descartes, Galileo).

Chapter 8 — The Men on the Cushions (p. 197) Official printing and government patronage. The States Printer (a post held by the van Wouw family for 75+ years) was one of the most lucrative positions in the trade. Over 100 towns, provinces, and official bodies issued ordinances, proclamations, and forms — all needing a printer. Official printing was steady, reliable income (if not always promptly paid). The chapter also covers the States General’s use of print for propaganda during wartime, and the development of the plakkaat (official proclamation) as a genre.

Chapter 9 — The Dangerous Pleasures of Leisure (p. 220) The True Freedom period (1650–1672) under Johan de Witt, and the rise of commercial leisure publishing. With the Stadhouder dormant, the regents promoted a culture of civic humanism. Publishing boomed: songbooks, plays (Vondel, Bredero, Hooft), novels, travel accounts, and the first Dutch newspapers flourished. The chapter covers the Second Anglo-Dutch War, the Raid on the Medway (1667), and how Dutch printers exploited patriotic sentiment. Also covers the Antiquarian and learned periodical market — the birth of the book review.

Chapter 10 — Art and Power (p. 242) Returns to Rembrandt’s bookshelf as a framing device. Rembrandt owned only 22 books at his bankruptcy — a contrast with his vast collection of props (antique guns, helmets, costumes). The chapter argues that Rembrandt “thought visually” and had little engagement with text. This opens a broader discussion of the relationship between art, text, and print — illustrated books, emblem books, maps, and the market for engravings. Covers the Blaeu family’s magnificent atlases, the Toonneel der Steden (city atlases), and the intersection of cartography, art, and commerce.

Chapter 11 — Bookshop of the World (p. 265) The international book trade. The chapter title echoes the book’s own — Amsterdam as the world’s book emporium. Covers the auction system (invented in the Dutch Republic), newspaper advertising of books, the export networks to England, Germany, Scandinavia, and the Baltic, and the role of the Dutch in supplying Latin books to the entire European scholarly community. The Elzeviers’ duodecimo classics and Blaeu’s atlases were the most visible exports, but the bulk of the trade was in everyday books: Bibles, schoolbooks, devotional works, and practical manuals.

Chapter 12 — The Art of Collecting (p. 294) Book ownership and libraries. Opens with a 1658 pamphlet by a minister complaining he couldn’t live on 500 guilders a year — his budget allowed only 25 guilders for books, paper, ink, and newspapers. Yet ministers somehow built substantial libraries. The chapter uses probate inventories to analyse book ownership across Dutch society: regents, ministers, merchants, artisans, and the very poor. The median collection size, the types of books owned, the role of auction catalogues, and the collecting mania for Elzeviers and other fine editions among the wealthy.

Chapter 13 — Boundaries (p. 321) The limits of Dutch tolerance. The book world was freer than anywhere in Europe, but there were boundaries. Blasphemy, sedition, and attacks on the established church were prosecuted. The chapter covers the censorship of Socinian and radical works, the case of Adriaan Koerbagh (imprisoned for his rationalist writings), and the prosecution of printers who violated privileges or published libels. Argues that Dutch tolerance was pragmatic rather than principled — a “protection racket” for Catholics, real limits for radicals.

Chapter 14 — Our Learned Friends (p. 347) The Republic of Letters in practice. How Dutch publishers served the international scholarly community — the Elseviers’ correspondence with scholars across Europe, the role of the Amsterdam book auctions in distributing learned books, and the birth of the learned journal (the Journal des Sçavans, the Philosophical Transactions, and their Dutch counterparts). Dutch printers produced works by Descartes, Hobbes, Galileo, Grotius, and Spinoza — often when their home countries forbade them.

Chapter 15 — The Business Press (p. 365) The Ramphuisjaar (Disaster Year, 1672) and its aftermath. The French invasion, the murder of the De Witt brothers, and the economic crisis devastated the book trade. Many printers went bankrupt; the auction market collapsed. But out of this came new forms — the business press, financial newspapers, and the first economic periodicals. The chapter covers the Amsterdamse Courant, Europische Mercurius, and the birth of financial journalism.

Chapter 16 — The Golden Trade (p. 390) Conclusion: the legacy of the Dutch Golden Age book world. The auction system, newspaper advertising, the duodecimo revolution, and the Republic of Letters all outlived the 17th century. The Dutch book trade’s innovations shaped European publishing for centuries. The chapter reflects on what was lost (the sheer scale of ephemeral print that has disappeared) and what endures (the collections, the catalogues, the infrastructures of knowledge).

Key Excerpts

Prelude (pp. 1–2)

When in 1656 Rembrandt was forced to declare bankruptcy, a full inventory was made of all of his remaining possessions. Among the paintings, furniture and household goods at the house on the Breestraat, were only twenty-two books… By this point the Dutch Republic was a land teeming with books. Its publishers produced some of the most fabulous books of the age. More of its citizens read and owned books than anywhere else in Europe… It is said that Dutch homes found space for 3 million pictures on their walls. Maybe, but they produced many more books, perhaps as many as 300 million. They traded at least 4 million books at auction.

Beginnings — Louis Elzevier (pp. 27–28)

In the autumn of 1580, a footsore tradesman named Louis Elzevier made his weary way through the gates of the city of Leiden. At the age of 33, Elzevier was no longer a young man, and he was definitely down on his luck. By this point he had tried virtually every occupation in the book trade: printer, bookseller, bookbinder. Even a period in the workshop of the famous publisher Christophe Plantin in Antwerp had not helped him find his feet; now after failed ventures in Liege and Douai he had turned his steps north to Leiden, a place of opportunity in the free north.

Even here he did not immediately succeed. A loan from his old employer Plantin hung like a sword of Damocles over his whole enterprise; when Plantin demanded repayment, Elzevier was forced to sell his house and shop to clear the debt.

News Cycles — Broer Jansz and the Execution of Oldenbarnevelt (p. 68)

Broer Jansz hurried down from Amsterdam to take his place in the silent crowd watching the aged statesman’s dignified end. What followed next was truly extraordinary. For Broer Jansz returned to Amsterdam, to publish not a pamphlet, as he would have done ten years previously, but a special issue of his weekly newspaper… demand was so high that he had to print two editions.

The Elzeviers and Galileo (p. 7)

When Galileo was forbidden to publish his work in Italy, it was the Leiden firm of Abraham and Bonaventura Elzevier that brought Two New Sciences to the press. Galileo contributed a preface personally thanking the Elzeviers for rescuing his work in this way.

The Scholarly Publishing Ecosystem (Ch. 7)

By the third decade of the seventeenth century it was abundantly clear that Amsterdam would play a dominant role in the new Dutch state… But in one respect at least Amsterdam played second fiddle: for it was Leiden, the home of Holland’s only university, which emerged as the intellectual powerhouse of the new state.

Relevance to the Dutch Republic Knowledge Base

This is one of the most important modern works on the Dutch book world, directly relevant to many existing pages:

  1. Dutch Knowledge Culture — The book’s central argument (books as the overlooked foundation of the Golden Age) directly supports and expands this concept. The statistical data alone (300M books, 4M at auction) is invaluable.

  2. The Elseviers (new entity) — Chapter 1 opens with Louis Elzevier’s arrival in Leiden; Chapters 4 and 11 cover the Abraham & Bonaventura partnership. Galileo’s Two New Sciences (Chapter 11) is a key fact.

  3. Plantin and Antwerp — Louis Elzevier worked for Plantin in the 1560s; Plantin’s loan nearly bankrupted him. Links the Southern and Northern Netherlands book trades.

  4. Oldenbarnevelt and the Pamphlet Wars — Chapter 2 covers the Remonstrant crisis as a print phenomenon. A key source for the religion-and-politics concept.

  5. VOC and Global Trade — Chapter 4 covers books on VOC ships, devotional literature for sailors. Links to voc-dutch-east-india-company.

  6. The Blaeus — Covered in Ch. 10 for their atlases and in Ch. 11 as Elsevier imitators. Cross-refs with joan-blaeu entity.

  7. Censorship and Tolerance — Chapter 13 directly addresses the limits of Dutch tolerance, the Koerbagh case, and the pragmatic nature of Dutch religious policy.

  8. Leiden University — Chapter 7 covers the academic book market, the Elseviers’ role, Leiden’s monopoly on higher education.

  9. The Economic Context — The Disaster Year (1672), the Anglo-Dutch Wars, and the decline of the book trade are covered in Part IV.

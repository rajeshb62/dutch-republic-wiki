Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma

Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma and Piacenza (1545–1592), Italian-born prince of the House of Farnese, was Governor-General of the Spanish Netherlands from 1578 until his death. Widely regarded as the ablest of Philip II’s governors, he was a brilliant commander whose military genius temporarily reversed the fortunes of the Dutch Revolt — yet whose achievements were repeatedly undermined by impossible instructions from Madrid. Motley portrays him as the “charioteer” best fitted to guide the “confusum chaos” of the Netherlands, a man who “united the unscrupulous audacity of a condottiere with the wily patience of a Jesuit.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Birth and Education

Alexander Farnese was born in 1545, the son of Ottavio Farnese, grandson of Pope Paul III, and Margaret of Parma, the natural daughter of Emperor Charles V. He was one of twins born to Margaret and her young husband. His great-grandfather, Pope Paul III (whose secular name was Alexander), placed his hand on the newborn’s head and prophesied he would become a mighty warrior. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

At age eleven, when Philip II was still in the Netherlands after the Emperor’s abdication, Alexander was given as a hostage for his parents’ friendship. He begged to serve as a volunteer at Saint Quentin and wept when refused. His education was completed at Alcala and Madrid under the supervision of his royal uncle, in the company of the Infante Don Carlos and the brilliant Don John of Austria. The imperial bastard alone could surpass the Italian prince in martial pursuits. At twenty he was affianced to Maria of Portugal, granddaughter of King Emanuel. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Youthful Character and Duelling Exploits

During enforced peacetime in Parma, the young prince’s restless martial spirit found outlet in nightly duels. Disguised and well-armed, he would perambulate the streets confronting every martial-looking passenger he encountered. He would seek out and challenge any gentleman whose bravery was commended in his hearing. This ended when he faced Count Torelli, whose torchlight revealed his opponent’s identity — Torelli dropped his sword, knowing that victory would earn the prince’s hatred and defeat his contempt. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Lepanto (1571)

When the Holy League was formed against the Turk, Alexander extorted permission from Philip and flew to the Levant. Don John received him joyfully just before Lepanto and gave him command of several Genoese galleys. Alexander laid his galley alongside the Turkish treasure-ship, sprang aboard wielding an immense two-handed sword, and cut a path through the enemy ranks. Mustapha Bey fell before his blade. The booty was prodigious — individual soldiers obtained two and three thousand ducats each. Don John commended his nephew but censured his rashness; Alexander replied that his saintly wife’s prayers had sustained him. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Assumption of Government after Don John

Don John of Austria, broken in health and spirits, died in October 1578 in a pigeon-house hovel, appointing his nephew Alexander as his successor. At thirty-three, Alexander Farnese became Governor-General. Motley writes that “of all the eminent personages to whom Philip had confided the reins of that most difficult and dangerous administration, the man who was now to rule was by far the ablest and the best fitted for his post.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Personal Appearance and Character

Less attractive but more commanding than Don John, Farnese was decorous and self-poised, passionate only before the enemy. He rarely permitted disrespect to escape deliberate chastisement. Motley’s portrait is striking: “Cool, incisive, fearless, artful, he united the unscrupulous audacity of a condottiere with the wily patience of a Jesuit. He could coil unperceived through unsuspected paths, could strike suddenly, sting mortally.” His personal habits were extremely temperate — he said he ate only to support life and rarely finished a dinner without rising three or four times for business. He attended mass regularly by torchlight in winter. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Union of Arras (1579)

Within six months of Farnese’s advent to power, he seized the double-edged sword of religious dissension and cut the Netherlands in twain. Through a combination of military pressure and dazzling personal diplomacy — including a magnificent ball where Parma himself danced with the Walloon envoys — he detached the Walloon provinces from the Patriot cause. The Union of Arras (January 1579) reconciled the Catholic southern provinces with Philip’s sovereignty, permanently dividing the Netherlands. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Siege of Maastricht (1579)

Parma invested Maastricht in early 1579. The siege became legendary for its brutality. When a mine explosion killed five hundred Spaniards and his officers begged him to recall the troops, Alexander cried out: “Go back to the breach and tell the soldiers that Alexander is coming to lead them into the city in triumph, or to perish with his comrades.” His generals physically blocked his path. The city fell after four months, and eight thousand inhabitants lay dead in the streets. Alexander entered in triumph, borne on a chair of state beneath a golden canopy, through “deserted streets of shattered houses” where “the pavement ran red with blood.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Siege of Antwerp (1584–85)

The siege of Antwerp — the greatest military operation of the age — was Farnese’s masterpiece. After the assassination of William of Orange in 1584, Parma systematically closed the Scheldt, reducing the greatest commercial city of Europe by blockade and engineering. The famous “Kowenstyn” dyke was breached; a massive floating fortress called the “War’s End” proved unmanageable. Antwerp capitulated on August 17, 1585. Marnix of Saint Aldegonde negotiated the surrender. Motley describes the siege as “one of the most brilliant military operations of the age and one of the most memorable in its results.” The terms were generous, but Antwerp never recovered its commercial supremacy — the mouth of the Scheldt remained in Dutch hands. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

The Armada (1588)

Farnese was appointed chief commander of the Holy League’s forces for the invasion of England. Philip II’s bull named “Alexander of Parma chief commander” of the enterprise against Elizabeth. Farnese assembled a fleet of three hundred transports and thousands of veterans, yet was kept “a close prisoner by the fleets of Holland and Zeeland.” The fatal flaw in Philip’s plan was that no provision had been made for the junction of Parma’s flat-bottomed boats with the ocean-going Armada. Parma repeatedly warned Philip that his small river-craft could not cross open water without protection, but was ignored. The Armada was scattered by storms and English fireships, and the invasion never materialized. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Master of Duplicity

Farnese conducted simultaneous peace negotiations with English envoys at Ostend while preparing for invasion. He personally reconnoitered Ostend disguised in plain livery as a rabbit-shooting servant. Philip wrote approvingly: “I see you understand me thoroughly. Keep the negotiation alive till my Armada appears.” Parma became fond of English greyhounds, sent as gifts from the English commissioners — Cecil noted “you would wonder how fond he is of English dogs.” Meanwhile, months were consumed in diplomatic procrastination. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Intervention in France (1590–92)

Twice Farnese marched into France to support the Catholic League against Henry of Navarre. In 1590 he brilliantly relieved Paris, outmaneuvering Henry at Lagny — an operation so skillful that Henry’s own generals confessed themselves out-generalled. In 1592 he raised the siege of Rouen. During his second French campaign, the two greatest captains of the age met face to face near Baroges. Alexander wore a felt hat, a great ruff, and a little furred cassock, mounted on an unarmored horse, galloping about to rally his carabineers. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Later Campaigns and Death

Returning from France broken in health, Farnese found his veteran troops unpaid and mutinous. In July 1591, Maurice of Nassau — the rising young commander of the Dutch — outmaneuvered him at Fort Knodsenburg, routing ten companies of Parma’s picked cavalry before his eyes. It was a sign of shifting fortunes. Philip II, suspicious and ungrateful, sent the Marquis of Cerralvo to recall his nephew, treating the greatest general of the age with “treachery.” Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, died at Arras on December 3, 1592, worn out by wounds, illness, and the weight of impossible expectations. Motley notes that “Philip the Prudent” had already begun secret preparations to replace him with the Archduke Albert. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Assessment

Motley judges Farnese as nearly the equal of William of Orange in ability — trapped by the same impossible task of reconquering a people unwilling to be conquered. “Had it not been for the courage, the vigilance, and the superior intellect of a single antagonist” — William the Silent — “the whole of the Netherlands would have shared the fate which was reserved for the more southern portion.” Farnese’s tragedy was that his military and diplomatic genius was spent in the service of a master who gave him too little support and too little trust. “The Duke of Parma,” Motley concludes, “was the ablest of Philip’s governors, but he was always the servant of a king who understood nothing and forgave nothing.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Johan van Oldenbarnevelt

Johan van Oldenbarnevelt (September 14, 1547 – May 13, 1619), also known as John of Barneveld, was Grand Pensionary (Advocate) of Holland and the civilian architect of the dutch-republic. For more than thirty years he was the foremost statesman of the Netherlands — the indispensable administrator, diplomat, and financial genius who transformed a desperate rebellion into a sovereign commonwealth. His rivalry with maurice-of-nassau over the supremacy of civil versus military authority, inflamed by the Arminian religious controversy, led to his arrest in 1618, trial before a packed tribunal, and execution on the Binnenhof in 1619. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Motley wrote that “if William the Silent was the founder of the independence of the United Provinces, Barneveld was the founder of the Commonwealth itself.” ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Early Life and Education

Barneveld was born in Amersfoort in 1547, of the ancient and knightly house of Oldenbarneveldt, of patrician blood through all his ancestors. He was not heir to large possessions and was a diligent student from youth upward, pursuing profound studies in the universities of Holland, France, Italy, and Germany. At an early age he became one of the first civilians of the time. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

He served as a volunteer at his own expense through several campaigns of the Revolt, nearly losing his life at the disastrous attempt to relieve Haarlem and being so disabled by sickness and exposure at the siege of Leyden that he was deprived of witnessing its triumphant conclusion. Successfully practising law afterwards before the tribunals of Holland, he was called at the age of twenty-nine to the post of Chief Pensionary of Rotterdam. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Motley describes him as “a man of noble and imposing presence, with thick hair pushed from a broad forehead rising dome-like above a square and massive face; a strong deeply-coloured physiognomy, with shaggy brow, a chill blue eye, not winning but commanding, high cheek bones, a solid, somewhat scornful nose, a firm mouth and chin, enveloped in a copious brown beard.” ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Advocate of Holland and Architect of the Republic

After the assassination of william-of-orange in 1584, Barneveld was foremost among the statesmen of Holland to spring forward and inspire the Republic with renewed energy. He was at the head of the embassies that offered the sovereignty first to England and then to France. Both monarchs refused the dangerous crown. When Elizabeth sent the Earl of Leicester as governor-general, Barneveld countered by proposing and carrying the appointment of Maurice of Nassau to the stadholdership of Holland, precisely to prevent the consolidation of the Provinces into a centralized union under English control. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Soon afterwards, the nobles and regents of Holland urged Barneveld to accept the post of Advocate of that province. After repeatedly declining, he accepted on the remarkable condition that if any negotiation should be undertaken to bring Holland back under the dominion of Spain, he should be considered from that moment relieved from the service. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Gradually, without intrigue, from “the commanding power of the man, the native authority stamped upon his forehead, he became the political head of the Confederacy.” He created and maintained a system of public credit “absolutely marvellous in the circumstances, by means of which an otherwise impossible struggle was carried to a victorious end.” ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Architect of the VOC

Oldenbarnevelt was the political force behind the creation of the United East India Company (VOC), overcoming fierce resistance from the Amsterdam monopolists who had petitioned the States of Holland in 1601 for an exclusive charter covering all Dutch East India trade. He led the opposition in the States of Holland, arguing that the Indies trade should benefit the whole country rather than a single town, however powerful. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt] The States of Holland resolved in October 1601 that “good order, supervision, and a single administration” were essential, and instructed its delegates to the States General to consult all the voorcompagnieen — not only Amsterdam’s but those of Middelburg, the Meuse, and the Northern Quarter as well. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

As moderator of the negotiations at The Hague in January 1602, Oldenbarnevelt warned the bickering companies that the King of Spain was “relishing this discord” and that the national interest demanded a united front. The compromise he engineered created six chambers (Amsterdam, Zeeland, Rotterdam, Delft, Hoorn, and Enkhuizen) overseen by a board of seventeen directors — the Heeren Zeventien — with eight from Amsterdam, four from Zeeland, two from the Meuse, two from the Northern Quarter, and a rotating seventeenth member to ensure Amsterdam never held a majority. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt] When Zealand stalled through February, Oldenbarnevelt travelled to Middelburg on March 3 and pressed the recalcitrant factions into agreement, backed by the timely presence of Prince Maurice. The charter was voted by the States General on March 20, 1602, granting the VOC a twenty-one-year monopoly over Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt] Dutch historians credit Oldenbarnevelt with bringing the merger to a conclusion in under five months; he later remarked that he risked 5,000 guilders of his own money in the Company “in order not to be taken solely for an advisor.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt]

The Twelve Year’s Truce (1609)

Barneveld’s greatest diplomatic achievement was the negotiation of the Twelve Year’s Truce with Spain, signed in the spring of 1609. After more than forty years of hard fighting, Spain virtually acknowledged the independence of the Republic. The truce was the work of the Advocate, but was “detested by Maurice and by Maurice’s partisans” — for a soldier whose genius had reclaimed the republic’s territory, peace meant idleness and political irrelevance. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

The Religious Controversy: Remonstrants vs. Counter-Remonstrants

During the Truce years, the theological dispute over predestination between the Arminians (Remonstrants, followers of Jacobus Arminius) and the Gomarists (Counter-Remonstrants, followers of Franciscus Gomarus) tore the Republic apart. Barneveld, who had always believed that the civil authority must be supreme over the church, championed the Remonstrant cause and the principle of provincial sovereignty (States’ rights) against the centralizing claims of the Synod. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Maurice, backed by the orthodox Calvinist clergy and the urban masses, became openly the chieftain of the Contra-Remonstrants. “The conflict between the civil and the military element for supremacy in a free commonwealth has never been more vividly typified than in this death-grapple between Maurice and Barneveld.” The aged statesman, “ripe with half a century of political lore,” faced the “high-born, brilliant, and scientific soldier, with the laurels of Turnhout and Nieuwpoort and of a hundred famous sieges upon his helmet.” ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Arrest, Trial, and Execution (1618–1619)

On August 29, 1618, as the Advocate walked to the session of the States of Holland within the Binnenhof, he was accosted by a chamberlain of the Prince and led to the Stadholder’s apartments. There Lieutenant Nythof of the Prince’s bodyguard arrested him in the name of the States-General. Grotius and Hoogerbeets were taken the same day. “Thus the three statesmen — culprits as they were considered by their enemies — were secured without noise or disturbance.” ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Barneveld was examined some sixty times before a special tribunal of twenty-four judges, all his political enemies, without counsel, without access to the charges, without the right to cross-examine witnesses. He was forced to draw up his own defence, “without books, notes of evidence, or even copies of the charges.” ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

On May 12, 1619, sentence of death was read to him. On the morning of May 13, after writing farewell letters to his wife and children in a spirit of “truly calm, courageous, and religious” composure, he was led to a hastily erected scaffold in the Binnenhof. He knelt on the bare planks, declared to the silent crowd that he died a good patriot, and said to the executioner: “Be quick about it. Be quick.” The head was struck off at a single blow. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

Legacy

Barneveld’s execution is the single most contested act in the political history of the Dutch Republic. Maurice never fully recovered from the stain on his reputation. The Advocate’s sons and allies, including Hugo Grotius (who escaped from prison in a book chest), kept his memory alive. His fundamental principle — the supremacy of civil over ecclesiastical authority — remains a cornerstone of liberal republican thought.

Motley judged him as one of the great unrecognised figures of history: “There have been few great men in any history whose names have become less familiar to the world, and lived less in the mouths of posterity.” His career had been “closely identical with a national history” — from the desperate defence of Haarlem and Leyden through the creation of the Republic’s financial system, the negotiation of the Truce, and the final cataclysm of his destruction by the military faction he had himself raised to power. ^[source: motley-barneveld.txt]

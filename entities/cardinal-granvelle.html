Cardinal Granvelle (Anthony Perrenot)

Anthony Perrenot (1517–1586), later known as Cardinal Granvelle, was a Burgundian prelate and statesman who served as the de facto chief minister of the Spanish Netherlands under Regent Margaret of Parma. The son of Charles V’s trusted minister Nicolas Perrenot, he rose rapidly through ecclesiastical ranks — becoming Bishop of Arras, Archbishop of Mechlin, and finally Cardinal — and became the architect of Philip II’s centralizing and absolutist policy in the provinces. John Lothrop Motley portrays him as a brilliant, industrious, and profoundly arrogant schemer whose secret correspondence with Philip II, his role in creating the new bishoprics, and his contempt for the Netherland nobility made him the primary target of noble resentment and the symbolic embodiment of Spanish oppression. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Early Life and Education

Granvelle was born in 1517 at Besancon in Burgundy (then part of the Holy Roman Empire), the eldest of thirteen children of Nicolas Perrenot, who had risen from an obscure Burgundian family to become the long-serving favourite minister and man of business to Emperor Charles V. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

He studied at Dole, Padua, Paris, and Louvain. By the age of twenty he spoke seven languages — Latin, French, German, Flemish, Spanish, and others — with perfect facility. His knowledge of civil and ecclesiastical law was considered prodigious. At twenty-three he became a canon of Liege Cathedral, the necessary eight quarters of gentility having been duly produced by his panegyrists. The rich bishopric of Arras was already prepared for him by his father’s care before he had reached the requisite age. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Early Career under Charles V

In 1543, at the Council of Trent, Granvelle delivered a learned and brilliant harangue that so charmed the Emperor that Charles V created him councillor of state. A few years later he rendered Charles still more valuable proofs of devotion and dexterity by the part he played in the memorable imprisonment of the Landgrave of Hesse and the Saxon Dukes. He was thereafter constantly employed in embassies and other offices of trust and profit. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Rise to Power

At the abdication of Charles V in Brussels (October 1555), Granvelle — then still Bishop of Arras — was already a conspicuous figure, described by Motley as “the serene and smiling priest whose subtle influence over the destinies of so many individuals then present, and over the fortunes of the whole land, was to be so extensive and so deadly.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

When Philip II departed for Spain in 1559, leaving his half-sister Margaret of Parma as Regent, the real administration of the Netherlands was placed in the hands of Anthony Perrenot. He became the chief of the Consulta, the secret council of three by whose deliberations the Duchess was to be governed. He was soon elevated to the cardinalate and became known throughout Christendom as Cardinal Granvelle. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Character and Methods

Motley paints a vivid portrait of Granvelle’s abilities and vices. He was ready-witted, smooth and fluent of tongue, fertile in expedients, courageous, and resolute. He thoroughly understood the art of managing men — particularly his superiors. He knew how to govern under the appearance of obeying. In his intercourse with Philip, “he was a chameleon to the hand which fed him,” colouring himself with the King’s character, anticipating his thoughts, and substituting protocols for conversation. “No flattery could be more adroit.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

His industry was enormous. He could write fifty letters a day with his own hand, dictate to half a dozen amanuenses at once on as many different subjects in different languages. He corresponded with Philip, with Margaret, with everyone — writing folios to the Duchess when they were in the same palace, pages-long letters to the King, and sending off multiple couriers on the same day. “Such prolixity enchanted the King.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

His political principles were those of a strict absolutist. He strenuously deprecated allowing the constitutional authorities any control over expenditures and warned Philip against assembling the States-General. He supported the re-enactment of the Emperor’s remorseless edict of 1550 — the “edict of blood and fire” — as the very first measure of Philip’s reign. He referred to the people as “that vile and mischievous animal called the people.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

His avarice was as notable as his ability. By 1557 his income from his see and other livings was estimated at ten thousand dollars annually, with property in ready money, furniture, and tapestry valued at two hundred and fifty thousand dollars — sums representing multiples of those figures in modern terms. He was, in Motley’s words, “a shameless mendicant of pecuniary favours and lucrative offices,” rebuked even by Charles V for his greed. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

The New Bishoprics Plan

Granvelle’s most controversial policy was the creation of fourteen new bishoprics for the Netherlands, with himself as Archbishop of Mechlin. The plan aimed to reorganize the ecclesiastical structure, tighten Spanish control over the Church, and provide a more effective instrument for suppressing heresy. The Pope issued the necessary bull at Philip’s request while the King was still in the Netherlands. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

The new bishoprics provoked fierce opposition from Orange, Egmont, and other influential nobles, who saw them as a device to introduce the Spanish Inquisition by stealth and to place the nobility’s patronage networks under ecclesiastical control. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Relations with the Nobility

Granvelle’s growing unpopularity was matched only by the mounting hostility of the great nobles. The relationship between Orange and Granvelle had once been one of friendship but gradually changed to enmity. Orange, Egmont, and Horn came to view the Cardinal as the instrument of Philip’s absolutist designs and as a spy reporting their every move to Madrid. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

The Fool’s-Cap Livery

The most famous episode of public contempt for Granvelle occurred at a banquet given by Caspar Schetz, the treasurer, in 1563. The assembled nobles, inflamed with wine, decided to invent a livery as different as possible from the Cardinal’s pompous equipage. Egmont won the dice-throw to devise the symbol, and soon the retainers of the house of Egmont appeared in coarse grey doublets and hose with an embroidered device resembling either a monk’s cowl or a fool’s cap and bells — aimed at Granvelle, who had been accustomed to call the nobles “zanies, lunatics, and buffoons.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

The fool’s-cap livery became the rage. All the frieze-cloth in Brabant was exhausted, all the serge in Flanders clipped into monastic cowls. Margaret of Parma at first laughed with the rest, but Granvelle ensured the King was informed. The Duchess obtained that the caps be omitted from the livery, but a bundle of arrows or a wheatsheaf was substituted — symbols that Granvelle darkly interpreted as confirming a conspiracy against the Crown. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Secret Correspondence and Recall

The three seigniors — Orange, Egmont, and Horn — withdrew entirely from the state council, refusing to serve alongside Granvelle. They wrote jointly to Philip demanding the Cardinal’s removal. Philip, in his characteristic fashion, responded with “elaborate duplicity.” He secretly wrote to Granvelle ordering him to leave the Netherlands on pretence of visiting his mother in Burgundy, while simultaneously writing public letters to the nobles expressing astonishment at their conduct and commanding them to return to the council. “The termination of the Granvelle regency,” Motley writes, “is most important… for the illustration which it affords of the inmost characters of the Cardinal and his ‘master.‘” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

On 13 March 1564, Granvelle departed Brussels. He left with a numerous suite and stately equipages, passing through the gate of Caudenberg where Brederode and Hoogstraaten, mounted together on one horse in exultation, watched him go. A placard appeared on his palace door reading “For sale, immediately.” Orange alone was not deceived by the elaborate mystification: “Although the Cardinal talks of coming back again soon,” he wrote, “we nevertheless hope that, as he lied about his departure, so he will also spare the truth in his present assertions.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Retirement in Burgundy

Granvelle retired to his charming estate at Orchamps in Burgundy, where he affected the life of a philosopher and Epicurean. He described his retreat in lyrical terms — “mountains high as heaven,” “rivers flowing through charming valleys, the waters clear as crystal, filled with trout” — and professed to wish himself blind and deaf to worldly affairs. In reality, Motley notes, “worldly affairs were his element, and he was shipwrecked upon the charming solitude which he affected to admire.” He let his beard grow to his waist, having vowed not to shave until recalled to the Netherlands — a return that never came. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Later Career

From his retirement, Granvelle continued to advise Philip through copious correspondence. After the death of Requesens (1576), he recommended a reappointment of Margaret of Parma as Regent, though his advice was not taken. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

He was later called to Rome, where he served as a cardinal of the Roman Church and was deeply involved in the affairs of the Inquisition. He died in 1586. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Motley’s Assessment

Motley’s verdict on Granvelle is severe. He acknowledges his profound learning, quickness, dexterity, and enormous industry, but judges that his “capacity for affairs” was always “the capacity for the affairs of absolutism.” He was “a clever, scheming politician, an adroit manager” — not a statesman. His character, Motley concludes, was “mainly compounded of pretence, like his whole nature and his whole life.” His relentless opposition to the constitutional liberties of the Netherlands, his role in intensifying religious persecution, and his contemptuous treatment of the nobility made him the catalyst that united the opposition and set the stage for the Dutch Revolt. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Relationships

  • Philip II of Spain — his master and correspondent, whom he served with “slavish” devotion while effectively directing policy from behind the throne
  • Margaret of Parma — the Regent he dominated as chief of the Consulta; their relationship was marked by mutual distrust beneath formal courtesy
  • William of Orange — his principal adversary, whose political genius Granvelle consistently underestimated
  • Count Egmont and Count Horn — open enemies whom Granvelle secretly denounced to Philip, and whose enmity contributed to his downfall
  • Viglius and Berlaymont — his allies in the state council, the “cardinalists” who remained loyal to his faction
  • Duke of Alva — the military enforcer whose appointment Granvelle helped prepare, and who recommended taking “several heads for treason” when Granvelle’s position became untenable
  • Champagny — his brother, who later became governor of Antwerp and suffered from the association with Granvelle’s hated name
  • Charles V — his first patron, under whose father (Nicolas Perrenot) he began his rise

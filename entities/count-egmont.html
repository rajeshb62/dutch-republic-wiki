Count Egmont (Lamoral, Count of Egmont)

Lamoral, Count of Egmont, Prince of Gavere (1522 – June 5, 1568), was a Flemish nobleman and general whose military victories made him the most popular hero of the Netherlands, and whose execution by the Duke of Alva made him a martyr of the Dutch Revolt. John Lothrop Motley portrays Egmont as a tragic figure — brave, gallant, and magnetic, but vain and politically naive, caught fatally between the cause of liberty and the demands of a treacherous crown. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Early Life and Family

Egmont was born in 1522, the younger son of John IV of Egmont and Francoise de Luxemburg, Princess of Gavere. The Egmont family claimed descent from the ancient Frisian kings Radbold and Adgildt, and through his mother’s line Egmont acquired some of the proudest titles and richest estates of Flanders. His elder brother Charles died young and unmarried, leaving Lamoral all the family estates and titles. As a youth he was a page of Emperor Charles V, and at age nineteen he served in the Emperor’s expedition to Barbary (1541), commanding a troop of light horse and distinguishing himself under the Emperor’s own eye for courage and devotion. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

His only sister married the Count of Vaudemont and became the mother of Louis de Vaudemont, later queen of Henry III of France. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Military Victories: St. Quentin and Gravelines

Battle of St. Quentin (August 10, 1557)

Egmont’s first great triumph came during the war between Spain and France. Philip II’s army assembled at Givet, and Egmont was the life and soul of the campaign. He was now in his thirty-sixth year, in the very noon of his brilliant life — “young, noble, wealthy, handsome, valiant,” with not a cloud yet on his horizon. At the Battle of St. Quentin, the French army under Constable Montmorency and Admiral Coligny was routed. The victory was complete, and though Philip’s hesitation allowed the French to escape total annihilation, Egmont emerged as the hero of the hour. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Battle of Gravelines (July 13, 1558)

The following year, Marshal de Thermes launched a foray from Calais into the Flemish frontier. Egmont, determined “to destroy De Thermes with all his force, or to sacrifice himself,” posted his army at Gravelines, midway between Calais and Dunkirk. Though suffering from severe sickness, he mounted his horse and personally led his troops to battle. “The foe is ours already,” he shouted as he charged; “follow me, all who love their fatherland.” His horse was shot under him at the start of the action, but he mounted another and pressed the attack. The battle became a savage hand-to-hand conflict on the sands, decided when English ships appeared offshore and a German cavalry detachment turned the French flank. The French army was annihilated — fifteen hundred fell in the action, as many were driven into the sea, and Marshal de Thermes himself was taken prisoner. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

The victory made Egmont the idol of the army and the people. The French ambassador Mondoucet would later remark, upon seeing Egmont’s head fall on the scaffold, that he had now seen “the head fall before which France had twice trembled.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Role in the State Council

After the Treaty of Cateau-Cambresis (1559), Philip II departed the Netherlands, leaving Margaret of Parma as Regent. Egmont was appointed a member of the Council of State alongside William of Orange and Count Horn. The triumvirate soon found themselves in opposition to Cardinal Granvelle, Philip’s chief minister, whose power and arrogance they resented. Egmont’s pride of rank and his fury at the insolence of the upstart cardinal made him a natural leader of the opposition. He joined Orange and Horn in writing a joint letter to Philip demanding Granvelle’s recall. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

The Fool’s-Cap Livery (December 1563)

At a dinner given by Caspar Schetz, Baron of Grobbendonck, in December 1563, the assembled nobles invented the famous “fool’s-cap livery” — a costume decorated with a fool’s cap or monk’s cowl, worn by their servants as a mockery of Cardinal Granvelle. The fashion became the rage of Brussels; all the frieze-cloth in Brabant and all the serge in Flanders was exhausted. The Duchess Margaret laughed at first, but Granvelle saw to it that Philip was informed. The costume was later modified, replacing the cowls with a bundle of arrows or a wheatsheaf — a symbol the nobles claimed represented union in the King’s service, but which Granvelle denounced as a conspiracy. Egmont himself dined at the Regent’s table after the Cardinal’s departure in a camlet doublet with hanging sleeves, the buttons stamped with the bundle of arrows. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Mission to Spain (1565)

In 1565, Egmont accepted a mission to Spain to plead directly with Philip II for moderation of the religious edicts and the abolition of the Inquisition. The council debate over his instructions was violent — Orange delivered a remarkable speech warning Egmont that Philip’s promises were worthless, and Viglius was so agitated he suffered an apoplectic fit. Philip received Egmont with apparent cordiality, flattered him, showered him with promises, and sent him back with letters full of affection. In reality, Philip had prepared two sets of notes for Egmont — one accepting his proposals, one politely declining — and left it to Margaret to choose which to deliver. Egmont returned to the Netherlands deceived and full of loyalty, convinced the King was his friend. Motley writes that “the transitory impression made upon his mind by the lofty nature of Orange was easily effaced in Spain by court flattery and by royal bribes.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol1.txt]

Repression in Flanders

After the Iconoclasm (Beeldenstorm) of August 1566, Egmont departed for his government of Flanders. Here he abandoned any pretense of standing with the national cause. As Motley describes it: “Egmont entered Flanders, not as a chief of rebels — not as a wise pacificator, but as an unscrupulous partisan of government, disposed to take summary vengeance on all suspected persons.” He ordered numerous executions of image-breakers and heretics. His secretary Bakkerzeel hanged twenty heretics, including a minister, at a single session. Flanders and Artois were filled with the wives and children of thousands who had fled the country to escape Egmont’s wrath. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

While Orange pursued a policy of conciliation at Antwerp, Egmont’s severity made him hated by the very people who had once looked to him as a potential champion. “Such achievements as these by the hands or the orders of the distinguished general who had been most absurdly held up as a possible protector of the civil and religious liberties of the country, created profound sensation.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Vacillation and Refusal to Flee

Throughout 1566-1567, Egmont wavered between the counsel of Orange and the demands of the Crown. He had generous instincts and the pride of high rank, but he was a fervent Catholic who regarded the image-breaking as an unpardonable crime. “We must take up arms,” he said, “sooner or later, to bring these Reformers to reason, or they will end by laying down the law for us.” When Orange warned him of the coming storm and urged him to arm or flee, Egmont refused. He could not believe that a king for whom he had won such victories could intend him harm. “Such loyalty he left to Egmont,” Motley writes of Orange, who chose exile. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

When the Duke of Alva arrived in August 1567 with a Spanish army, Philip had already written Egmont a personal letter full of affection and confidence to prevent him from taking alarm. Egmont rode to meet Alva at Tirlemont, presenting him with horses. Alva coldly observed to his attendants, loud enough for Egmont to hear: “Behold the greatest of all the heretics.” Yet he rode arm-in-arm with the unsuspecting Count into Brussels. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Arrest (September 9, 1567)

On September 9, 1567, after a banquet given by Alva’s natural son Don Fernando, Egmont and Horn were lured to a council on the Antwerp citadel plans. At the council table, Alva excused himself, and soldiers entered to arrest the Counts. Egmont surrendered his sword, remarking bitterly “that it had at least rendered some service to the King in times which were past.” He was confined in a black-draped chamber. His secretary Bakkerzeel and the burgomaster of Antwerp, Van Straalen, were arrested the same day. On September 23, Egmont and Horn were removed to the castle of Ghent, where they remained imprisoned for nine months. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Trial and Execution (June 5, 1568)

After Louis of Nassau’s victory at Heiligerlee (May 23, 1568), Alva hastened the sentence. On June 1, eighteen prisoners of distinction were executed on the Horse Market. On June 3, Egmont and Horn were brought from Ghent to the Brood-huis on Brussels’ Grand Place. The Bishop of Ypres was sent to inform them of their death sentence.

Egmont received the news with astonishment rather than dismay, having never believed in a fatal termination. He protested his loyalty, wrote letters to Philip and Alva professing his innocence, and spent the night in prayer after cutting the collar from his doublet and shirt himself so the hangman would not have to touch his person. His celebrated letter to Philip read: “I have never had a thought, and believe myself never to have done a deed, which could tend to the prejudice of your Majesty’s person or service.” He begged only that the King have compassion on his wife and eleven children. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

On the morning of June 5, 1568, three thousand Spanish troops were drawn up around a black-draped scaffold on the Grand Place. Egmont walked from the Brood-huis with a steady step, reading aloud Psalm 51. He was dressed in a robe of red damask over a short black mantle embroidered in gold, with a black silk hat and black and white plumes. On the scaffold he expressed bitter regret that he was not permitted to die, sword in hand, fighting for his country and his king. Sanguine to the last, he asked Julian Romero whether a pardon was not even then to be granted — Romero shrugged, murmuring a negative. Egmont gnashed his teeth, then recovered himself, threw aside his robe, took the badge of the Golden Fleece from his neck, and knelt. He said the Lord’s Prayer aloud and asked the Bishop to repeat it thrice. After receiving the crucifix and the Bishop’s blessing, he drew a cap over his eyes and cried, “Lord, into Thy hands I commit my spirit.” The executioner severed his head at a single blow. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The French ambassador, watching from a secret place, whispered that he had seen “the head fall before which France had twice trembled.” Tears were seen even on Alva’s face as he watched from a window opposite the scaffold. Horn was executed immediately after. The heads were exposed on iron stakes for two hours. The bodies were eventually conveyed to Saint Gudule, and Egmont’s remains were interred at Sottegem in Flanders. It was widely believed the heads were sent to Madrid in boxes so Philip could look upon the dead faces of his victims. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Assessment

Motley’s verdict on Egmont is unsparing but sympathetic: “Egmont is a great historical figure, but he was certainly not a great man.” His execution, Motley argues, remains “an enduring monument not only of Philip’s cruelty and perfidy but of his dullness” — the King had everything to hope from Egmont and nothing to fear. A man who “by a little management, might have been converted into a most useful instrument for the royal purposes” was instead made into a martyr, a victim, and a popular idol. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Egmont’s name became a rallying cry throughout the Revolt. The Pacification of Ghent (1576) and subsequent acts of union invoked his fate as a warning against Spanish tyranny. His widow and eleven children were left in such poverty that Alva himself wrote to Philip recommending an alms for them, noting he doubted “if there were so wretched a family in the world.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Posthumous references in the later volumes recall Egmont’s fate as a cautionary tale. His execution is referenced in Vol. III as one of the foundational grievances that drove the provinces toward the Act of Abjuration. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Relationships

  • William of Orange — fellow grandee in the State Council, whose counsel of prudence Egmont tragically ignored
  • Duke of Alva — his executioner, who lured him to arrest and condemned him to death
  • Philip II of Spain — his sovereign, who betrayed him with false promises of affection
  • Count Horn — his companion in arms and in death, executed on the same scaffold
  • Cardinal Granvelle — his rival in the State Council, object of the fool’s-cap livery mockery
  • Margaret of Parma — Regent of the Netherlands, who secretly defamed Egmont to Philip
  • Bishop of Ypres — his confessor in the final hours
  • Julian Romero — the Spanish officer who led him to the scaffold

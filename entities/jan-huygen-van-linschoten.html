Jan Huygen van Linschoten

Jan Huygen van Linschoten (1563 – February 8, 1611) was the Dutch traveller, merchant, and spy whose Itinerario (1596) laid open the Portuguese maritime secrets of the East Indies and provided the navigational intelligence that propelled the Dutch Republic into Asian trade. His published sailing directions, charts, and commercial data directly enabled the first Dutch voyages to the Indies and the formation of the Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie (VOC). ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

Early Life and Departure for India

Linschoten was born in 1563 in Haarlem, where his father was an innkeeper. Before he was ten, the family moved to Enkhuizen, a thriving Zuiderzee port whose bustling overseas trade filled the young Linschoten with wanderlust. At sixteen he joined his two stepbrothers in Seville, writing later: “With their help I wanted to get some experience about that country and a knowledge of the language.” One brother secured him a place in the retinue of a Spanish grandee travelling to Lisbon following Philip II’s annexation of Portugal. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

Service in Goa (1583–1588)

In Lisbon, Linschoten’s resourceful stepbrother Willem Tin learned that a new archbishop, Dom Frei Vincente da Fonseca, had been appointed to Goa. Willem wangled the post of escrivao (clerk) and succeeded in having Jan taken along in a minor capacity. The ships left Lisbon in April 1583 — more than nine years would pass before Linschoten returned to his native land. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

Based in Goa, the capital of the Portuguese empire in Asia, Linschoten spent five years absorbing everything he could see and hear. He never travelled further east than Goa itself, but Goa was the hub through which every traveller in the vast Portuguese network passed, and “the inquisitive Hollander had been adept at acquiring information.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary] He noted the dilapidated state of Portuguese defences, the corruption from viceroy down, and the fact that “everyone, including the clergy, is out for himself.” He lamented his own poverty: “If only I had a hundred ducats, I could easily make six or seven times that much.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

The Itinerario (1596)

Linschoten left Goa in 1589 on a Portuguese ship, but stopped in the Azores to work for an Antwerp merchant, finally reaching Enkhuizen in September 1592. There he found his mother, brother, and sister in good health, and soon fell in with the town surgeon Bernardus Paludanus, a renowned collector of rarities. The two men collaborated on what became Linschoten’s masterpiece. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

The full title was Itinerario, Voyage, or Passage by Jan Huygen van Linschoten to East or Portugal’s India, published in 1596. It was a monumental work: “covering just about every aspect of India conceivable in that period” — with Goa as its focal point, India stretching westward to Ethiopia and encompassing all of Southeast Asia, Sumatra, Java, and the Spice Islands. Republished in modern times with annotations, the Itinerario fills five heavy volumes. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

Sailing Directions and Commercial Intelligence

The Itinerario answered the central question Dutch merchants faced: what goods to send in exchange for spices. Linschoten reported that Portuguese ships carried little cargo to the Indies — mainly ballast and bullion — and that “pieces of eight” (Spanish silver reals) were legal tender throughout the known world. Since the Dutch received a steady flow of reals from trade with Spain, they could use the same silver to compete with the enemy in Asia. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

His sailing directions were equally critical: he advised crossing the Atlantic as far as the Abrolhos off Brazil before turning toward the Cape of Good Hope — a route that reduced the risk of being becalmed off Africa. He catalogued every suitable “refreshing” place from the Canaries to Madagascar, described the best seasons for crossing the Indian Ocean, and warned of the appalling conditions on Portuguese ships where “most of the crew are inexperienced people who have never before been to sea.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

Maps and the Northeast Passage

The Itinerario’s maps, prepared in consultation with the cartographer Petrus Plancius, were revolutionary. Linschoten’s map of the Northeast Passage — showing a conjectural route north of Russia past Nova Zembla and Cape Tabin (Cape Chelyuskin) into the Pacific — directly inspired Willem Barentsz’s three Arctic voyages. Linschoten had reported that Japan “is a cold land with much rain, snow and also ice,” leading Dutch geographers to conclude it lay at roughly Holland’s latitude and could therefore be reached by sailing north and then east. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, sections: Linschoten and His Itinerary, North to the Indies]

The part of the Itinerario dealing specifically with the route to the Indies was printed in advance of the rest of the work, almost certainly at the behest of the Amsterdam merchants. A copy was carried on the first Dutch fleet that sailed for the Indies in 1595. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: South to the Indies]

Bestseller and Translations

The Itinerario soon became a bestseller. A second edition followed quickly, and within two years it was translated into English (by William Phillip in 1598) and German. Latin and French editions followed. “Within a few years, all of western Europe knew as much about the Indies as the Dutch.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, sections: Linschoten and His Itinerary, The English East India Company]

The Northern Voyages (1594–1595)

When the States General organised the first expedition to find a Northeast Passage in 1594, Linschoten sailed with the Enkhuizen ship under Cornelis Nay, charged with keeping a careful record “from day to day, and hour by hour, of all that we encountered.” Willem Barentsz commanded the Amsterdam ship, following Plancius’s advice to sail due north past Nova Zembla — and found only ice. Nay, however, sailed through the Kara Strait into an ice-free Kara Sea, raising hopes that the passage was open. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: North to the Indies]

Linschoten was summoned to The Hague to report in person to Prince Maurice and Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, and convinced them that the Kara Sea was “a ready-made and certain passage.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: North to the Indies] A second northern expedition followed in 1595, with Linschoten serving as “Chief Merchant.” This time the Kara Sea was choked with ice — the voyage failed. Linschoten remained a believer in the route, pointing out that it had taken the Portuguese many years before they finally rounded the Cape of Good Hope. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Second & Third Voyages North]

Exclusion from the VOC and Later Life

When the first Dutch fleet to sail around the Cape was assembled in 1595, Linschoten was conspicuously absent from the roster, despite having twice sailed around the Cape. The reasons were a tangle of rivalries: Plancius regarded Linschoten’s knowledge as “second-hand information” and the two men clashed — Linschoten dismissed Plancius as a “dogmatic preacher” who “tries to prove that this route is neither good nor possible” with “thousands of spurious reasons.” Plancius, who had invested 5,400 guilders in the company and trained its navigators, had the final say in personnel. Moreover, Linschoten belonged to Enkhuizen while Plancius was committed to Amsterdam, and the advance-printed route section of the Itinerario made his physical presence unnecessary. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: South to the Indies]

Linschoten returned to Enkhuizen, his fame secure. He died on February 8, 1611. His name is perpetuated by the Linschoten Vereeniging (founded 1908), the Dutch counterpart of the English Hakluyt Society, which publishes original accounts of Dutch voyages. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, section: Linschoten and His Itinerary]

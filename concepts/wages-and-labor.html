Dutch Wages and Labor

The Dutch Republic developed Europe’s most distinctive preindustrial labor market, combining the continent’s highest wages, a deeply segmented structure of employment, mass immigration, and elaborate poor-relief institutions. Between 1580 and 1620, the Republic’s labor market experienced what De Vries calls an “explosion of demand and supply” that set it fundamentally apart from the rest of Europe. The wage evidence is consistent not with Malthusian stagnation but with an economy where heavy investment expanded employment opportunities and raised productivity. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.1-12.5]

Nominal and Real Wages

De Vries compiled the most comprehensive wage series for the Dutch Republic, drawing on construction labor at ten locations in the Western Netherlands (Holland, Utrecht, Zeeland) and seven in the Eastern Netherlands (Tables 12.1-12.2). In the mid-sixteenth century, master carpenters and masons earned 7-8 stuivers per day across most of Holland, 5 stuivers in the eastern cities. Unskilled laborers earned 3.5-4.5 stuivers in Holland and 2.5-3 in the east. After 1570, especially in the bursts of 1572-83 and 1592-1616, wage increases became frequent and substantial — a drainage board at Spaarndam raised wages eight times in these periods. By the last quarter of the seventeenth century, master craftsmen in the west earned at least 28 stuivers per day, while in the east the rate was 20 stuivers. Unskilled labor earned at least 18 stuivers in the west and 12 in the east. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.2]

Once this pattern was attained, wage rigidity set in dramatically. Some daily wage rates remained unchanged for 200 years and were not increased substantially until 1870. The official pay scales for VOC employees — seamen, shipwrights, soldiers — did not change from 1636 to the Company’s demise in 1795. Admiralty wharf pay scales were unchanged from at least 1695 until 1780. This rigidity extended well beyond construction: salaries for municipal employees, Reformed ministers, school teachers, and orphanage staff all show the same post-1650 stability. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.2]

East-west wage differentials persisted throughout the three centuries. Wages in the Eastern Netherlands rose more rapidly than in the west up to the eve of the Revolt, reaching 70-75 percent of the western level. Thereafter, explosive growth in the maritime region caused eastern wages to fall back. After 1620, eastern craftsmen recovered ground, but unskilled laborers were rarely paid more than two-thirds of the average western wage until 1740. Winter wage reductions — typically 20-25 percent below summer rates, paid for longer periods after 1650 — further compressed annual earnings. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.2]

The work year itself changed dramatically. The Calvinist Synod of Dordrecht in 1574 abolished all holidays except Sunday, plus Easter Monday, Ascension Day, Pentecost Monday, and Christmas — extending the potential work year from about 260 to 307 days, a 15 percent increase in labor supply. By the early seventeenth century, pay records show individuals routinely laboring over 300 days per year, yielding some 3,600-3,800 annual working hours. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.2]

Real wages — nominal wages deflated by a basket-of-consumables cost-of-living index (Figure 12.4, Tables 12.4-12.5) — followed a distinctive four-phase trajectory. From 1500 to 1560, prices rose 240 percent while wages rose only 144-156 percent, causing severe deterioration. In the period 1575/9 to 1615/19, however, prices rose 165 percent while wages surged 225-268 percent, more than recovering lost ground. After a brief setback in the 1620s, real wages recovered to a peak in the 1680s. From the 1680s-1730s plateau, a steady decline set in, reaching its nadir after 1800. From the 1660s onward, real wage movements were primarily price-driven. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.4]

The Dutch real wage trajectory diverged dramatically from the rest of Europe (Table 12.3, Figure 12.6). Between 1580 and 1620, while Dutch real wages rose vigorously, English real wages fell by half. This “Dutch exceptionality” — the avoidance of the pan-European real wage erosion of the price revolution — opened an enormous gap that lasted until after 1730, when English money wages rose periodically while Dutch wages remained frozen, closing the gap entirely by about 1800. Salaried workers (ministers, teachers, municipal employees) followed a different path: their compensation fell during the sixteenth-century price inflation but recovered sharply after 1550, and by 1800 stood 26 percent higher than in 1664, even as day wages stagnated. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.2, 12.4]

Despite surface rigidity, employers found covert ways to adjust compensation. In the late eighteenth century, the Rijnland drainage board raised annual earnings by 22 percent between 1784 and 1800 without ever changing the posted daily wage — by paying workers for five work periods (schoften) instead of four on summer days. Signing bonuses, New Year’s “gratifications,” and beer-money adjustments served the same purpose elsewhere. This “hidden wage drift” complicates the picture of complete rigidity. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.5.3]

The Segmented Labor Market

After the mid-seventeenth century, the Dutch labor market crystallized into three distinct segments. The “core” segment comprised large public and semipublic organizations — Admiralty ship wharves, VOC harbor facilities, drainage authority maintenance works, municipal public works departments — plus guild-organized crafts and capital-intensive enterprises (mills, refineries, ship wharves). These offered desirable, reasonably secure jobs at high wages that employers maintained even as demand diminished, likely following an efficiency-wage logic that allowed selection of the best workers and reduced turnover. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.5.2]

The second segment consisted of casual, seasonal, and labor-intensive employment concentrated in construction, agriculture, peat digging, whaling, and fisheries. Demand in this sector became intensely seasonal — passenger barge traffic on the Amsterdam-Haarlem trekvaart shows a sharp winter decline after 1650 (Figures 12.10-12.11), a “temporal contraction” as employers idled their least productive capacity. The unraveling of the domestic seasonal employment system made it increasingly difficult for laborers to assemble a full year’s work. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.5.2]

The third segment was the VOC, functioning as an “employer of last resort.” The Company’s workforce doubled between 1680 and 1740. Nearly 20 percent of each cohort of twenty-year-old men born in Holland died abroad in VOC service. Recruitment generated a complex “service industry” of zielverkopers (“soul sellers”), mostly women, who housed and maintained penniless recruits before enlistment. By the 1660s, half of VOC employees were foreigners; the Dutch share rose to 73 percent around 1700 as domestic unemployment grew, then fell to 35 percent by the 1770s. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.5.2]

Guilds and Labor Organization

Guilds played a central but tightly supervised role in the Republic’s labor markets. In Amsterdam, the number of guilds doubled in the century after the Revolt, and a third of the city’s adult male labor force were guild members. In Leiden, 52 guilds covered about 11,000 persons — one-fifth of the city’s population including dependents — though many journeymen and apprentices were excluded. The patrician urban magistracies always kept firm control, using guilds as policy tools to shape population, set entry costs, and differentiate fees by birthplace and religion. In the boom period of the early seventeenth century, municipalities competed to attract craftsmen; after mid-century, tighter regulation became the norm. By the eighteenth century, guild rules often amounted to outright protectionism, restricting entry, granting children favored access, and regulating the local labor market to the advantage of the settled citizenry. Guild funds (gildebossen) provided sickness, unemployment, and widow’s benefits, distributing perhaps 200,000 guilders annually to several thousand members and survivors in Amsterdam. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 5.2, Ch. 8, Ch. 11.5]

Skill Premiums and Occupational Structure

The skill premium underwent dramatic shifts. From 1460 to 1550-70, unskilled wages deteriorated from 67 to 55 percent of the skilled wage. But in the rapid expansion after 1580, the relative position of the unskilled improved to 75 percent by 1630-50 (Figure 12.7), a graphic demonstration of rapid economic expansion pressing upon labor supply. After 1680, the trend reversed: the unskilled-to-skilled ratio declined from at least 70 percent to no more than 61 percent after 1755. The wages of sawyers — sensitive to investment levels in construction, shipbuilding, and farm improvements — rose from 90 percent of masters’ wages to a peak of 130 percent by 1610, before Cornelis Cornelisz.’s invention of the wind-powered sawing mill in 1596 drove them into technological unemployment; the Amsterdam sawyers’ guild was disbanded in 1627. Occupational structure varied sharply by region (Table 11.3): the maritime provinces were far more industrialized, with a larger share of wage-dependent labor. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.5.1, Ch. 11.2]

Labor Mobility and Immigration

Massive immigration shaped the Dutch labor market from the start. Holland’s population nearly doubled between 1570 and 1620, its urban population rose from about 40 to nearly 60 percent, and the effective nonfarm labor supply grew by nearly 3 percent per year — natural increase, immigration, proletarianization, and the Calvinist abolition of dozens of religious holidays (extending the work year from about 260 to 307 days) all combined to flood the Republic with wage labor. Immigrants filled specific occupational niches: bakers were mainly Germans, as were many bricklayers and stonemasons; tailors and shoemakers came from Gelderland, Overijssel, and adjacent German districts; textile workers from the Southern Netherlands. The Hollandgdngerei brought 10,000 or more Westphalian hannekemaaiers annually from the late seventeenth century onward, crossing the border on foot for seasonal work in peat bogs and hayfields. After 1682, when the States of Holland required “acts of indemnity” certifying that migrants would not become public charges, mobility was obstructed — Amsterdam alone refused to require them. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 3.5, Ch. 12.5.1-12.5.2]

Female and Child Labor

Women’s work was both extensive and largely unseen. Women performed heavy physical labor in salt pans, brickworks, breweries, peat bogs, and bleaching fields near Haarlem. Municipal ordinances in Gouda required hemp hackling halls to keep windows closed against the particulate generated by female workers. Amsterdam’s grain warehouses employed zaadverschietsters to turn grain, and Utrecht formed a women’s guild with the exclusive right to pull boats through the city’s canals. Yet the Goldberg Enquete of 1801 (Table 11.28) found only 15 percent of 505 firms employed women (concentrated in textiles), suggesting relatively low female formal-sector participation — possibly a consequence of the Republic’s high living standards and its pioneering ideal of middle-class domesticity (gezelligheid). Child labor was far more widespread: 47 percent of firms employed children. The pipe makers of Gouda used one or more children per master, perhaps 1,000 children at the industry’s peak. The VOC’s orphanage-linked recruitment was widespread; entrepreneurs repeatedly proposed mass child-labor schemes for manufacturing, though most failed. By the late eighteenth century, workhouses in at least forty cities sought to employ the poor and their children in what De Vries calls “the beginning of a new attitude toward child labor.” ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 11.6]

Income Redistribution and Poor Relief

The Republic’s poor-relief system was elaborate but not redistributive in the modern sense. After the Revolt, Church property was expropriated, and each city asserted authority over charity. Parallel poor-relief agencies existed for Reformed, Remonstrants, Walloon Reformed, Roman Catholics, Anabaptists, Lutherans, and Sephardi and Ashkenazi Jews. A nationwide count in 1829 identified 2,855 poor-relief agencies and 403 institutions. Charitable expenditures probably reached 8-10 million guilders before the 1811 bond default — 3-4 percent of national income — concentrated in the maritime cities. The Reformed diaconate’s spending rose from 40,000 guilders around 1600 to nearly 500,000 in the 1750s, even as eligibility tightened (residence requirements rose from two to six years). In Amsterdam, 10-12 percent of households received winter support (Figure 12.15), though this rarely exceeded one-third of an unskilled worker’s weekly earnings and was only for winter months. The system was designed for seasonal fluctuations, not structural crises. After 1770, rising prices and unemployment overwhelmed it, and the crise de subsistence — long absent thanks to the international grain trade — reappeared. Reformers proposed workhouses as alternatives to outdoor relief, but most achieved little. As De Vries concludes, the system held as a “safety net” sufficient to mitigate the most conspicuous forms of need — the dikes held, “just barely, for the poor.” ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.6]

Exceptionality Lost

The Dutch advantage eroded after 1750 and disappeared by 1800. Across Europe, commodity prices rose while money wages remained sticky; the Republic, with frozen wage scales and segmented labor markets, was as vulnerable as any. By the 1770s, Dutch workers, even when unemployed, proved reluctant to enlist with the VOC or Admiralty, creating a paradox of labor shortage alongside growing pauperism. The Netherlands remained rich long after it had ceased being prosperous, and its legacy of labor market segmentation, poor-relief practices, and entrenched rentier and commercial interests posed a formidable obstacle to the new economic initiatives needed to restart modern economic growth in the nineteenth century. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt, Ch. 12.5.3, Ch. 12.6]

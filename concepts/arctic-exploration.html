Dutch Arctic Exploration — The Search for a Northeast Passage

**The Dutch Republic’s search for a northeast passage to Asia (1594–1597) produced some of the most dramatic polar exploration of the sixteenth century. Though the passage was never found, the voyages of Willem Barentsz discovered Spitsbergen, achieved the first European overwintering in the Arctic, and opened the whaling grounds that became a cornerstone of the Dutch Golden Age. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 10-12]

Background and Preparations

Following English attempts by Willoughby, Chancellor, and Burrough in the 1550s, Dutch merchants in Enkhuizen and Amsterdam took up the search. The exiled Antwerp merchant Balthasar de Moucheron submitted a plan to Prince Maurice in 1593, proposing to find the passage and fortify the Kara Strait. The States General ordered the admiralties of Zealand and West Friesland to fit out ships, while Amsterdam, spurred by the cartographer Petrus Plancius, contributed its own vessel. Plancius argued for a bold northern route — sailing due north past Novaya Zemlya, across the Pole if necessary — reasoning that rivers emptying into the Kara Sea would cause coastal ice. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 10]

The First Expedition (1594)

On June 5, 1594, three ships sailed north. The admiralty vessels under Cornelis Nay carried Jan Huygen van Linschoten as chronicler; they entered the Kara Sea through the Strait of Vaigach (renamed Strait Nassau) and found open water as far east as the mouth of a river, returning convinced they had found the gateway to Cathay. The Amsterdam ship under Willem Barentsz, following Plancius’s northern route, found “great store of ice, as much as they could descry out of the top, that lay like a plain field of ice.” After sailing some 1,500 miles and changing course more than 80 times, Barentsz was forced to turn back. The fleet returned on September 16, 1594 — one ship with bad news, the other with good. Linschoten reported to Prince Maurice and Oldebarnevelt that the Kara Sea was a “ready-made and certain passage.” ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 10]

The Second Expedition (1595)

Flush with optimism, the States General backed a second voyage with seven ships from Amsterdam, Hoorn, Enkhuizen, Middelburg, and Rotterdam. Cornelis Nay commanded again, with Barentsz as chief navigator; Linschoten went as chief merchant. The fleet left Texel on July 2, 1595 — much later than the previous year — and found Strait Nassau blocked by ice. The Kara Sea was full of it. Landing parties encountered Samoyeds, hungry polar bears, and decaying whales that spread a putrid stench. A small mutiny broke out; five ringleaders were hanged. After almost six weeks of frustration, the council ordered a retreat. The fleet limped home in mid-October. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 12]

The Third Expedition (1596–97)

Only Amsterdam was still willing to pay for a third attempt. On May 18, 1596, two ships sailed: Barentsz commanded one with Jacob van Heemskerck as captain, while Jan Ryp skippered the other. After reaching Novaya Zemlya, Barentsz and Ryp quarrelled over the route. Ryp insisted on continuing north, following Plancius’s advice — and in doing so they discovered Spitsbergen (Svalbard). But Barentsz refused to go further that way and returned to Novaya Zemlya. Ryp turned for home. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 12]

Barentsz sailed north along the coast of Novaya Zemlya, rounded its northernmost point (the Hook of Desire), and for a while found open water that promised passage. Then the ice closed in. By September the ship was locked solid at Ice Haven (76°N). The crew of sixteen built a cabin from driftwood and ship’s timber — Het Behouden Huys (the Safe House) — measuring 30 by 18 feet. They lived in it for eight months through the polar winter, recording their ordeal in a journal kept by Gerrit de Veer. They celebrated Three Kings’ Day with a feast, shot a polar bear that gave “a hundred pounds of fat,” and suffered through cold that nearly suffocated them. The ship was crushed by ice. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 12]

The Death of Barentsz

On June 14, 1597, the survivors launched two open boats and sailed 1,600 miles along the coast, dodging ice. A few days out, a dying Barentsz asked de Veer: “Lift me up, Gerrit. I want to see that land once more.” Three days later — June 20, 1597 — he died. Led by Heemskerck, the crew reached the Kola Peninsula at the end of August, where they encountered Jan Ryp on a trading mission. Twelve of the sixteen men survived. They appeared before the Amsterdam merchants wearing “fur caps made of white foxes.” ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 12]

Legacy: Discovery, Whaling, and the Cabin

Gerrit de Veer’s journal was published promptly, translated into several languages, and became an epic of navigation. Spitsbergen had been discovered; they had reached 81° latitude, further north than anyone before. They had been the first Europeans to survive a winter in the Arctic. But the only economic advantage proved to be the whales they had sighted around Spitsbergen and Novaya Zemlya. The Dutch quickly developed a thriving whaling industry in Arctic waters — by 1614 the Greenland Company had received a charter, and Dutch whalers clashed with the English Muscovy Company over fishing rights. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 12 and Book III]

In 1871, the Norwegian whaler Captain Elling Carlsen discovered the Safe House on Novaya Zemlya, perfectly preserved after nearly 300 years. An Englishman, Charles Gardiner, visited in 1876. Both brought back clothes, tools, and utensils now held in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam — a haunting testament to the men who had sought the northeast passage and instead opened the Arctic to European enterprise. ^[source: raw/masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, Masselman, Book I, Ch. 12]

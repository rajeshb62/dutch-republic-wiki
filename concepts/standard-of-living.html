Dutch Standard of Living and Welfare

The standard of living in the seventeenth-century Dutch Republic was among the highest in Europe, driven by vigorous economic growth, the highest real wages on the continent, and a uniquely elaborate system of poor relief and welfare institutions. Yet prosperity was distributed unevenly — the rich grew dramatically richer while a substantial underclass survived at subsistence level. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

Wages and Employment

Between 1580 and 1620, wages in Holland rose threefold. Real wages increased by 20–40 per cent depending on occupation, training, and location, with wages in Holland significantly higher than those in Overijssel or Drenthe. After 1640, wage growth levelled off into a two-century plateau. Comparisons with London and Paris show that while an unskilled building worker’s purchasing power in pounds of bread was roughly comparable, Dutch workers benefited from two structural advantages: a stable food supply guaranteed by Baltic grain imports, and an apparently inexhaustible demand for labour driven by the VOC, the textile and sugar-refining industries, and the armed forces. Domestic labour supply could not keep pace — half a million immigrants settled in the Republic across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, making up 6–8 per cent of the population. In Amsterdam, a third of those marrying were immigrants, and in Leiden, barely 44 per cent of bridegrooms had been born in the Republic. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

Wage Series (1500–1815)

De Vries and van der Woude compiled the most comprehensive wage series for the Dutch Republic, based on construction labour at ten locations in the Western Netherlands (Holland, Utrecht, Zeeland) and seven in the Eastern Netherlands — Tables 12.1 and 12.2. In the mid-sixteenth century, master carpenters and masons earned 7–8 stuivers per day across most of Holland (9–10 in Haarlem), 6 stuivers at Bergen op Zoom and Utrecht, and as little as 5 stuivers in the eastern cities of Gelderland and Overijssel. Unskilled labourers earned 3.5–4.5 stuivers in Holland, 3 in Utrecht, and 2.5–3 in the east. The skill premium (the excess of skilled over unskilled wages) rose from 50–67% around 1460 to over 80% by 1520–50. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

After 1570, and especially during the bursts of 1572–83 and 1592–1616, wage increases became frequent and substantial. A drainage board at Spaarndam raised wages eight times in these periods. By the last quarter of the seventeenth century, master craftsmen in the Western Netherlands earned at least 28 stuivers per day; in the eastern and northern provinces the most common rate was 20 stuivers. Unskilled labour earned at least 18 stuivers in the west, 15 in intermediate locations such as Utrecht, and 12 in eastern towns. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Once this pattern was attained, wage rigidity set in dramatically. Some daily wage rates remained unchanged for 200 years and were not increased substantially until 1870. The official pay scales for VOC employees — from seamen to shipwrights — did not change from 1636 to the Company’s demise in 1795. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Cost of Living

The cost of living index constructed by De Vries and van der Woude (Figure 12.4, covering 1480–1819) was based on a basket of consumables providing over 14,000 kcal per day: rye (1,050 kg), yellow peas (143.5 kg), beef (100 kg), cheese (50 kg), butter (50 kg), beer (621 L), peat (100 turf tons), and industrial products weighted to equal 25% of the food subtotal in the 1575–99 base period. After 1750, beer in the basket was replaced by coffee, tea, and gin. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Real Wages and Dutch Exceptionality

Real wages — nominal wages deflated by the cost of living index — followed a distinctive four-phase trajectory (Table 12.6). From 1500 to 1560, prices rose 240% while wages rose only 144–156%, causing a sharp deterioration of purchasing power. In the period 1575/9 to 1615/19, however, prices rose 165% while wages surged 225–268%, more than recovering the lost ground. A brief setback in the 1620s was followed by recovery to a peak in the 1680s. After the 1680s–1730s plateau, real wages entered a steady decline that reached its nadir after 1800. From the 1660s onward, real wage movements were primarily price-driven: falling prices lifted purchasing power, and later rising prices crushed it. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

The Dutch real wage trajectory was fundamentally different from the rest of Europe. Between 1580 and 1620, while Dutch real wages were rising vigorously, English real wages fell by half. This “Dutch exceptionality” — the avoidance of the pan-European real wage erosion of the price revolution — opened an enormous gap between Dutch and English purchasing power that lasted well into the eighteenth century. After 1730, the Dutch advantage wasted away as English money wages rose periodically while Dutch wages remained frozen at their mid-seventeenth-century level. By about 1800 the gap had disappeared entirely. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

GDP and National Income

De Vries and van der Woude provide the first systematic national income estimates for the Republic. Using the Personeele Quotisatie of 1742 — a careful tax assessment of (almost) all urban Hollanders with annual incomes above 600 guilders — they estimate per capita income in Holland at 152 guilders (Table 13.1). After correcting for underreporting and excluded income, the revised estimate for Holland rises to 180–198 guilders per capita. Across the entire Republic, per capita income in 1742 stood at 134–142 guilders, with total national income between 264.9 and 279.3 million guilders. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Key structural features of this economy emerge: the total wage bill accounted for roughly 45% of national income; interest income from government bonds, concentrated in the maritime cities, represented 8% of the national total but nearly 15% of income in those cities; total provincial and federal taxes absorbed 12–15% of national income; retained imports represented about 25%; and agricultural output, in the 70–80 million guilder range, made up 25–30% of total income. Holland, with 40% of the Republic’s population, generated 54–56% of national income and paid over 60% of taxes. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

For 1800–10, correcting the Physiocratic estimates of W. M. Keuchenius yields a per capita income of about 162 guilders at current prices (versus Keuchenius’s 116), or 168–206 guilders in 1910 prices. However, the cost of living rose at least 40% between 1742 and 1800–10, so the apparent increase in per capita income was illusory: in real terms the Republic had stagnated or declined. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Comparisons with Britain (Figure 13.1) show that Dutch per capita income stood at roughly 1720–44 price levels, approximately matching or exceeding British levels. Britain’s per capita income grew only 7.5% to 1800 and 25% by 1830; the Republic’s lead in 1760 may not have been eliminated until about 1800. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Social Stratification

The wealth gap widened markedly. In Leiden, the top 1 per cent owned one-fifth of all capital around 1600, one-third by 1623, and fully one-half by 1700. The middle class — those with assets of 1,000–10,000 guilders, like the Amsterdam furrier Hermanus Verbeeck who earned around 400 guilders a year — saw their average wealth stagnate and even decline slightly after mid-century. The wealthy (30,000 guilders and above) grew in number and saw their assets rise by 40 per cent. Most wealth concentrated in Holland, and within Holland in Amsterdam: almost half of the Republic’s 250 richest people lived there, their fortunes drawn overwhelmingly from trade and the VOC rather than land. The working class formed 35–50 per cent of the urban population; the middle class 20–30 per cent; the poor 10–15 per cent, plus roughly 10 per cent in domestic service. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

The Personeele Quotisatie of 1742 reveals the income hierarchy of Holland’s sixteen cities (Table 11.13). Amsterdam stood at the top, followed by four large cities (Haarlem, Leiden, The Hague, Rotterdam), then five middle-sized cities (Delft, Dordrecht, Enkhuizen, Gouda, Hoorn), and six small cities. Among the under-600-guilder majority, De Vries and van der Woude reconstruct a sketch of household income distribution (Table 11.12): the “Lesser Burgerij” at 350–600 guilders (35% of households), the “Working class” at 300–350 guilders (30%), and “Het Grauw” at 200–300 guilders (15%). The average income for all households earning under 600 guilders was about 363 guilders per year. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Poor Relief and Welfare Institutions

The Republic’s system of poor relief was admired across Europe. Sir William Temple wrote in 1673 that “charity seems to be very National among them.” Unlike England, which levied poor taxes, the Dutch funded relief through monthly door-to-door collections. Each city administered its own system, often in partnership between civic authorities and the Reformed Church’s Board of Deacons. The Reformed poor received alms from church deacons; others depended on municipal relief. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

Alms packages typically included rent-free housing, medical care from municipal doctors, free schooling for children, bread, peat for heating, and occasional clothing. The Slackebaarts of Zwolle — three generations of widows and children — survived on a combined income of roughly 100 guilders a year (one-quarter of a middle-class household’s income) from sewing, child labour, and a mix of municipal and church alms. Permanent charity recipients made up about 6 per cent of Zwolle’s population, with another 4 per cent receiving temporary aid, proportions low by European standards. In some cities the poor reached 15 per cent. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

Income Redistribution and the Scale of Charity

De Vries and van der Woude provide the most detailed quantitative picture of the Republic’s charitable sector. The Reformed diaconate’s expenditures rose from 40,000 guilders per year around 1600 to 240,000 in 1645–49, to over 300,000 in the 1670s, and to nearly 500,000 in the 1750s — despite increasingly restrictive eligibility (a residence requirement that rose from two years to six by the early eighteenth century). ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

In Amsterdam, where parallel poor-relief agencies existed for Reformed, Remonstrants, Walloon Reformed, Roman Catholics, Anabaptists, Lutherans, and Sephardi and Ashkenazi Jews, the municipal authorities spent 400,000–500,000 guilders of tax revenue annually to support those in need, with private endowments and church collections generating as much again. Amsterdam was exceptional: alone among Dutch cities, it did not require “acts of indemnity” from migrants, and its municipal poor relief expanded as church charities restricted aid to permanent residents. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

The scale of charitable income transfers was substantial but not redistributive in the modern sense. A nationwide count in 1829 identified 2,855 poor-relief agencies and 403 institutions housing the poor and orphaned. Expenditures on poor relief stood at 3.5 million guilders in 1829, plus 1.5 million for institutions in 1815, but these figures postdate the government bond default of 1811 (the tiercering) which slashed endowment income. Before that crisis, charitable endowments — with a face value approaching 100 million guilders — probably yielded annual expenditures closer to 8–10 million guilders, representing 3–4% of mid-eighteenth-century national income. Every year the Republic’s orphanages housed 10,000–15,000 children at a cost of well over 1 million guilders. Guild funds (gildebossen) distributed perhaps 200,000 guilders annually to members and survivors. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Despite its scale, charitable support was modest per household — 15–20 guilders per year — and was designed as an income supplement rather than a full subsistence guarantee. In Amsterdam, the winter relief provided by the Oudezijds Huiszittenhuis (Figure 12.15) rarely exceeded one-third of an unskilled worker’s weekly earnings, even for families with four children. Outdoor relief had a strongly seasonal character: 10–12% of Amsterdam households received support in winter, but the number fell sharply in summer. ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

Guild Welfare and Civic Orphanages

Guilds provided sickness and unemployment benefits through pooled funds (gildenbossen). The Amsterdam wine brokers’ guild gave 3 guilders a week to sick members, enough to keep a family off public charity. Other guilds offered 1.5 guilders. These funds were insufficient for full subsistence but helped maintain middle-class status. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

Orphanages embodied the Republic’s stratified welfare: the Civic Orphanage (Burgerweeshuis) accepted only children of citizens (guild members by definition), feeding them a varied diet of meat, fish, dairy, and eggs and training boys in skilled trades. The Almoners’ Orphanage (Aalmoezeniersweeshuis) housed poorer orphans, fed them half the calories, and sent them out as servants or day labourers — many ended up in the VOC. Mortality among poor orphans was more than five times that of citizen orphans. The Amsterdam Civic Orphanage even owned the city’s playhouse, using its proceeds to fund operations. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

Poverty and Vulnerability

Poverty had a clear gender dimension: widows like the Slackebaarts made up a disproportionate share of charity recipients. Textile workers and soldiers’ families were also overrepresented among the poor. Day labourers without guild membership had no insurance and relied on the “makeshift economy” — errands, guarding gates, or petty crime. The Amsterdam Rasphuis (house of correction, 1589) was a penal institution for beggars that became a tourist attraction, though most such houses closed within twenty years due to cost. The climatic “Little Ice Age” made winters long and cold, with peat for fuel an essential welfare item. Despite its achievements, the seventeenth-century Republic saw increasing social inequality that presaged troubles to come. ^[source: raw/prak-dutch-republic-17th-century.txt]

After 1770, the balance between charitable supply and demand broke down. Rising prices and structural unemployment overwhelmed a system designed for regular seasonal fluctuations. The crise de subsistence — long absent from the Republic thanks to its international grain trade, broad-based diet, and efficient interregional transport — reappeared. The value of poor-relief distributions rose while real wages fell, provoking elite complaints about “voluntary leisure” and a perceived “culture of poverty.” Reformers proposed workhouses as an alternative to outdoor relief, but most of these schemes — attempted in at least forty cities — achieved little. The Republic’s poor-relief system held, as De Vries and van der Woude put it, “just barely, for the poor.” ^[source: raw/de-vries-woude-first-modern-economy.txt]

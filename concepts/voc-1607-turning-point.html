1607: The Burgeoning Year

1607 was the pivotal year in which the Dutch Republic simultaneously broke Spanish sea power, compelled Spain to sue for peace for the first time, and launched the fleet that would cement the VOC’s position as an Asian territorial power. It is the year Dutch historians call “The Burgeoning Year” — a twelve-month span that reshaped the balance of power in both Europe and Asia. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, line 7580]

The chapter opens with a parallel: in the same year that English Puritans fled to Holland and John Smith founded Jamestown, the Dutch set in motion events that would build an eastern empire. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7570-7598]

Spanish Peace Overtures

After nearly forty years of war, Spain — still the most powerful nation in Europe — opened secret peace talks. On February 6, 1607, the merchant Werner Cruwel arrived at the house of Cornelis Aerssens in The Hague bearing a letter from Father Jan Neyen, a Franciscan monk in the service of Archduke Albert and Isabella. Neyen, born in Antwerp, was described by Hugo Grotius as having “an open face which inspired confidence but actually disguised a most wily and unscrupulous disposition.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7614-7692] Oldebarnevelt, awakened late that night, arranged a secret interview in Ryswyck with Prince Maurice as witness — the Stadholder, who had never known peace, was against negotiations from the start. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7643-7652] The Archdukes offered a ten-to-twelve-year truce on condition the Dutch suspend all activities in the Indies. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7662-7674] Amsterdam’s merchants, led by Reynier Pauw, vehemently opposed any truce, and on March 22 the city council resolved that peace “can only lead to an irreparable damage and eventual demise of these lands and its people.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7862-7865] Nevertheless, Oldebarnevelt pushed forward; an eight-month armistice was concluded on May 4, 1607, suspending hostilities in Europe but leaving the Indies question open. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7924-7930]

Battle of Gibraltar (April 25, 1607)

Before the armistice could take effect, a Dutch admiralty fleet of 26 warships under Jacob van Heemskerck engaged the Spanish home fleet in the Bay of Gibraltar. The Spanish had 21 ships, including ten of the largest galleons afloat, commanded by Don Juan Alvarez d’Avila. Heemskerck’s strategy was to attack in pairs, each pair boarding a galleon from both sides: “Hold your fire until you hear the hulls crack.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7941-7976] When d’Avila asked a captured Dutch skipper what the approaching ships wanted, the reply “It looks to me as if they want to fight” drew only a laugh — d’Avila boasted his flagship alone could take the lot. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7978-7985] Heemskerck was mortally wounded early in the battle — a salvo from the St. Augustine severed his leg at the groin — but ordered his captain Pieter Verhoef to conceal his death and continue. By dusk every Spanish vessel had been sunk or burned; 2,000–3,000 Spaniards died including d’Avila. The Dutch lost no ships and roughly 100 men, among them Heemskerck and the towering captain ‘Lange Hendrik’ of Amsterdam. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7994-8007] “The battle at Gibraltar proved to be one of the turning points in history: it spelled the end of Spain as a first-rate sea power, and the Dutch were ready to step into her place.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 8008-8010]

The Verhoef Fleet: Racing the Peace Freeze

Mindful that a permanent truce would freeze territorial holdings in Asia, the Seventeen secretly resolved to dispatch a powerful fleet before negotiations concluded. Command passed to Pieter Verhoef, the captain to whom Heemskerck had delegated command as he lay dying. The Seventeen drew up two sets of instructions: one set, made public, indicated nothing more than normal trade; the other, sealed orders to be opened past the Spanish coast, commanded territorial seizure. The fleet comprised 13 vessels — eleven commissioned by the VOC chambers and two contributed by the States General, signalling official approval of the real mission. It sailed in December 1607. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 9936-9969]

Matelief and the Ternate Alliance

Cornelis Matelief, a former VOC director commanding the Company’s third fleet (eleven ships, sailed May 1605), laid the diplomatic groundwork in the Moluccas. After a failed four-month blockade of Malacca and the loss of two ships, he learned that the Spanish had overrun Tidore and Ternate, capturing King Said Barkat — a staunch Dutch ally — and carrying him off to Manila. The entire Moluccas was threatened. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7027-7073] Matelief rushed east, reinforced Amboyna’s garrison, and built Fort Malayo. The Sultan of Ternate — nominal overlord of the clove-producing islands — granted the Dutch exclusive rights to purchase cloves in exchange for military protection against the Spanish entrenched on Tidore. The Dutch fixed the price at 50 reals per bahar and instituted a system of advances on future deliveries, paid in cotton goods. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 12528-12535] This treaty-monopoly framework — defense for exclusivity — became the template for the VOC’s entire spice regime.

The VOC’s Transformation into an Asian Power

The cumulative events of 1607 transformed the VOC. Gibraltar destroyed Spanish naval credibility, clearing the Indian Ocean for Dutch expansion — a victory Masselman calls “of greater importance than that gained over the Armada twenty years earlier.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 9916-9918] The peace talks compelled the Seventeen to think territorially rather than commercially for the first time. Matelief’s Ternate alliance gave the Company a legal monopoly over the world’s clove supply, enforced by Fort Malayo. And the Verhoef fleet — the largest the VOC had yet assembled, sailing under sealed orders of conquest — was explicitly tasked with seizing enemy territory before any status-quo freeze could take effect. The VOC was ceasing to be a merchant consortium and becoming a colonial state-in-formation, one that would rule the Indies for three centuries. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 7600-7613]

Jan Coen’s First Voyage

Among the sub-merchants aboard Verhoef’s fleet was a 22-year-old from Hoorn named Jan Pieterszoon Coen, hired at 35 guilders per month plus keep. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 9905-9908] He had been training under Dominie Plancius, studying celestial navigation and the Company’s secret voyage reports locked in the East India House. The departure was set for late December 1607; the Zuiderzee froze over immediately after the ships reached Texel, where the crew endured bitter cold waiting for a favorable wind. ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 10167-10173] Masselman calls Coen’s departure “his own unsung departure for the Indies during the last month of the year” and “the beginning of modern colonialism.” ^[source: masselman-cradle-of-colonialism.txt, lines 9922-9925] Coen would return as Governor-General, founding Batavia and building the Dutch empire in Asia.

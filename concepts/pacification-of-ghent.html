Pacification of Ghent (1576)

The Pacification of Ghent (signed November 8, 1576) was a treaty of union among all seventeen provinces of the Netherlands, forged in the aftermath of the Spanish Fury at Antwerp. It united Holland and Zeeland with the other fifteen provinces in a common league to expel the Spanish soldiery, abolished the Inquisition and the heresy placards of Charles V, and recognized the Reformed religion as the established creed of Holland and Zealand while preserving Catholicism in the loyal provinces. It was the high-water mark of Netherlandish unity during the Revolt, and the immediate precursor of the Union of Utrecht (1579). ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

The Spanish Fury and the Congress at Ghent

On November 4, 1576, Spanish mutineers —— unpaid and leaderless after the death of Requesens —— sacked Antwerp in an orgy of massacre and plunder known as the Spanish Fury. Eight thousand persons were killed and twelve millions in property destroyed —— more dead than in the St. Bartholomew’s Day Massacre in Paris. The atrocity shocked even the Catholic provinces into solidarity with the rebels. A congress representing nearly all the provinces convened at Ghent. William the Silent, through his deputies (including Saint Aldegonde), directed the negotiations from Middelburg. The Ghent citadel surrendered on the same day the treaty was signed. Simultaneously, Zeeland was recovered from Spanish control by Count Hohenlo, and on the very eve of the massacre, Don John of Austria —— the hero of Lepanto —— arrived in disguise at Luxemburg, a few days too late to prevent the union. ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Key Provisions (25 Articles)

The treaty contained twenty-five articles. It was subscribed at Ghent on November 8, 1576, by Saint Aldegonde and eight other commissioners appointed by the Prince of Orange and the estates of Holland on the one side, and by Elbertus Leoninus with other deputies from Brabant, Flanders, Artois, Hainault, Valenciennes, Lille, Douay, Orchies, Namur, Tournay, Utrecht, and Mechlin on the other. It established a mutual forgiving and forgetting of past offences; pledged a close and faithful friendship; and vowed the joint expulsion of the Spaniards. Upon that expulsion, a states-general on the model of the assembly that witnessed the abdication of Charles V was to be convened to settle the affairs of religion in Holland and Zeeland. Full liberty of communication and traffic between the provinces was guaranteed. Those of Holland and Zealand were not to attempt anything outside their territory against the Roman Catholic religion, nor on that account to injure or irritate anyone by deed or word. All placards and edicts on heresy, together with the criminal ordinances of the Duke of Alva, were suspended until the states-general should otherwise ordain. The Prince of Orange was to remain lieutenant, admiral, and general for his Majesty in Holland, Zealand, and associated places until the states-general provided otherwise. All prisoners, including the Comte de Bossu, were to be released without ransom. Restitution of estates and property not yet alienated was promised. ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

The Religious Settlement

The treaty’s religious provisions were its most delicate and most consequential element. Catholicism was recognized as the state religion of the fifteen loyal provinces, while the Reformed faith was formally established in Holland and Zeeland —— the two provinces that had borne the brunt of the war. In the Catholic provinces, private Reformed worship was not to be prohibited. The infamous placards of Charles V and the ordinances of Alva —— the legal machinery of persecution —— were suspended. The Inquisition was abolished. This was not a declaration of universal toleration, but it opened the door. As Motley observed, “an immense stride had been taken, by abolishing the edicts, and prohibiting persecution.” The arrangement was deliberately ambiguous: the Prince of Orange hoped that time and the return of exiled Protestants would gradually strengthen the Reformed cause in the Catholic provinces, while the Catholic negotiators hoped that the reservation of public worship to their faith would preserve the old order. Both expectations were in part disappointed. ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Character and Significance

The Pacification was a masterpiece of diplomacy by William of Orange. As Motley wrote, “the Ghent treaty, largely interpreted, opened the door to universal toleration.” The treaty was received with a shout of joy throughout the country —— proclaimed in every marketplace, ratified not by votes but by hymns of thanksgiving, triumphal music, thunder of cannon, and the blaze of beacons across the Netherlands. William the Silent saw it as “the prize of his life-long labors.” For a brief moment, the whole strength of the nation was enlisted against the foreign soldiery, and the divisions of a decade seemed healed. ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Fragility and Collapse

The unity proved fragile. Don John of Austria, having arrived too late to prevent the Pacification, spent the following months trying to undo it. The Perpetual Edict (February 1577) ostensibly ratified the Ghent treaty, but in practice isolated Holland and Zeeland from the other fifteen provinces. William the Silent refused to recognize the Edict, warning the states-general that the government was not acting in good faith and that secret preparations were being made to annihilate the authority of the states. Religious divisions deepened: the Union of Brussels (January 1577) had already begun closing the door to toleration that the Pacification had left open, sanctioning the Roman religion and animating Catholics to fresh persecution. By 1579, the Union of Utrecht in the north and the Union of Arras in the south formalized the permanent partition of the Netherlands along religious lines. The Pacification of Ghent, which had briefly united Catholic and Protestant against a common foe, became the “two-sided shield” that each party invoked against the other. As Parma’s agents noted, it was necessary to “pretend a respect for the Pacification” even while working to undo it; Saint Vaast declared flatly that “through the peace of Ghent all our woes have been brought upon us.” ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

Legacy

Though short-lived as a practical union —— it held for barely three months before Don John’s arrival began to unravel it —— the Pacification of Ghent was the foundational compact of the Revolt. It established the principle that the provinces could act together against royal authority, that religious persecution could be suspended by political agreement, and that the states-general, not the crown, could adjudicate the religious settlement. The Union of Utrecht (1579) expressly described itself as a strengthening of the Pacification, and the Act of Abjuration (1581) drew on the political logic the Pacification had set in motion. For William of Orange, it was the bridge over which the country stepped from subjection to sovereignty —— “He and his brothers, with their labor, their wealth, and their blood, had laid down the bridge over which the country had stepped to the Pacification of Ghent.” The treaty remains the signal demonstration that, for a brief moment, the seventeen Netherlands were capable of a united front against Spanish rule. ^[source: raw/motley-dutch-republic-vol3.txt]

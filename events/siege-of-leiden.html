Siege of Leiden

The Siege of Leiden (October 1573 – October 1574) was one of the most famous sieges of the Dutch Revolt. The city endured two successive investments — first under the duke-of-alva and then under Francisco de Valdez — and was ultimately relieved by the novel expedient of cutting the dykes and flooding the polders, allowing Admiral Boisot’s fleet of Sea Beggars to sail across the land to the city’s rescue. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

First Siege (Oct 1573 – Mar 1574)

The first leaguer began on October 31, 1573 under the Duke of Alva. It lasted until March 21, 1574, when Alva’s troops were summoned away to defend the frontier against the invasion of Louis of Nassau. The citizens, neglecting the advice of william-of-orange, failed to use the breathing space to victual the city or strengthen the garrison, reckoning over-confidently on Louis’ success. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Second Siege Begins

After Louis’ catastrophic defeat at the battle-of-jemmingen and death at Mookerheyde, Valdez reappeared before Leiden on May 26, 1574, at the head of 8,000 Walloons and Germans. The city was thoroughly invested — no fewer than 62 redoubts girdled the walls. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The military commandant was John Van der Does, Seigneur of Nordwyck, a noted scholar and poet. The civil authority was held by Burgomaster Adrian Van der Werf, whose steadfastness became legendary. The city’s garrison consisted only of a small corps of freebooters and five companies of the burgher guard — the main reliance was on the stout hearts of the inhabitants and the sleepless energy of William the Silent. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Investment

Valdez secured Maeslandsluis, Vlaardingen, and the Hague. Five hundred English troops under Colonel Edward Chester abandoned the fortress of Valkenburg and fled toward Leiden, but were refused admittance by the citizens (who distrusted them) and surrendered to Valdez. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Prince wrote to the citizens urging them to hold out at least three months, promising relief within that time. The city responded courageously. Requesens published a royal amnesty on June 6, 1574, inviting all repentant subjects to return to the Catholic church — it was received with universal contempt. Only two men in all Holland accepted: a brewer in Utrecht and the son of a refugee peddler from Leiden. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Starvation

By the end of June, the city was placed on strict rations: half a pound of bread and half a pound of meat per man per day. Sorties were frequent — a bounty was offered for every Spaniard’s head brought inside the gates — but eventually the authorities forbade further sorties lest the city lose its few disciplined defenders. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

As the summer wore on, famine tightened its grip. Bread and malt-cake disappeared; horses, dogs, cats, and rats were eaten. The hides of slaughtered cattle, chopped and boiled, were greedily devoured. The daily mortality was frightful — infants starved on maternal breasts withered by famine. Several thousand citizens perished. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Cutting of the Dykes

Orange, headquartered at Delft and Rotterdam, concluded that only the sea could save the city. The battle of Mookerheyde had foreclosed any possibility of land relief. His plan — to pierce the dykes along the Meuse and Yssel, open the great sluices at Rotterdam, Schiedam, and Delftshaven, and flood the polders — met resistance on economic grounds. His eloquence prevailed: “Better a drowned land than a lost land,” cried the patriots. The Estates consented by mid-July. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

On August 3, 1574, Orange went in person along the Yssel to Kappelle and superintended the rupture of the dykes in sixteen places. The ocean began to pour over the land. Some 200 vessels of various sizes were assembled at Rotterdam, Delftshaven, and other ports. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Prince’s Illness

Orange was struck down by a violent fever at Rotterdam. He lay prostrate, his mind agitated nearly to delirium by the unceasing schemes he was constructing. From his sickbed he continued to dictate letters of encouragement to the city and minute directions to Admiral Boisot. A false rumor that Leiden had fallen aggravated his condition. He began to mend only when the rumor was contradicted, and by the first week of September was convalescent. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Relief Fleet

Admiral Louis Boisot arrived from Zealand on September 1 with 800 veteran sailors — wild Zealanders, scarred and maimed from years of conflict, wearing crescents in their caps inscribed “Rather Turkish than Popish.” More than 200 vessels were assembled, carrying ten cannon each and manned by 2,500 veterans. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The distance from Leiden to the outer dyke was nearly fifteen miles. The fleet made its way to the Land-Scheiding, a strong dyke five miles from the city. On the night of September 10–11, this dyke was taken by surprise and the Spaniards on it killed or driven off. The patriots broke through in several places and sailed into the interior. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Advance

A second barrier, the Green-way, was similarly seized and levelled. A third, the Kirk-way, blocked the fleet at North Aa. The waters fell to just nine inches, stranding the armada. Day after day the fleet lay motionless. Orange, rising from his sickbed, came on board, reconnoitred the ground in person, and issued orders for the destruction of the Kirk-way. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

On the night of October 1–2, a violent equinoctial gale from the north-west piled the waters of the North Sea onto the coast. The fleet at North Aa suddenly had more than two feet of water. The Kirk-way was completely overflowed. The fleet sailed at midnight through the storm, sinking Spanish sentinel vessels. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Spanish Retreat

By dawn on October 3, the fleet had reached the last obstacle — the fortress of Lammen, bristling with artillery and swarming with troops. Boisot anchored and prepared for a desperate assault. But the fort was silent. A solitary boy was seen waving his cap from the battlements. The Spaniards had fled during the night, panic-stricken. Valdez, retreating from Leyderdorp, had ordered Colonel Borgia to abandon Lammen. The same night, a section of the city wall between the Cow-gate and the Tower of Burgundy had collapsed — the Spaniards believed it was a sortie. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Relief

The fleet swept past Lammen and entered Leiden’s canals on the morning of October 3, 1574. Bread was thrown from every vessel. The starving population snatched at it — some choked themselves to death in their greediness. A solemn procession formed immediately to the great church, where Admiral Boisot led the congregation in a thanksgiving hymn. So great was the emotion that the hymn was suspended as the multitude wept. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

The Prince arrived in Leiden the next day, despite warnings that the pestilential air might threaten his convalescence. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

Aftermath

The siege demonstrated that the Dutch could not be conquered by military means alone. As Motley writes, the Spaniards had been “vanquished, not by the enemy, but by the ocean.” ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

As a reward for the city’s heroism, the Prince and the Estates granted Leiden a ten days’ annual fair without tolls or taxes, and — more enduringly — established the University of Leiden, founded by charter in the King’s name (the fiction of Spanish sovereignty being maintained) and solemnly inaugurated on February 5, 1575, with a magnificent procession and classical pageantry. The university was endowed from the revenues of the ancient abbey of Egmont. ^[source: motley-dutch-republic-vol2.txt]

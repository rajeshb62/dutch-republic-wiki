Battle of Nieuport

The Battle of Nieuport (also Nieuwpoort) was fought on July 2, 1600, on the sand beaches and downs near the Flemish town of Nieuport. Maurice of Nassau, commanding the army of the States-General, defeated a Spanish force under Archduke Albert of Austria in one of the most dramatic tactical reversals of the Eighty Years’ War. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Prelude

The expedition was a project of Johan van Oldenbarnevelt and the States-General, who determined in June 1600 to invade Flanders, capture Nieuport, and from there redeem Dunkirk. The objective was to cripple the Flemish privateer bases that preyed on Dutch commerce. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Maurice opposed the plan as inexcusably rash, as did Sir Francis Vere and Lewis William of Nassau, who prophesied that the archduke would follow within a fortnight with an army capable of destroying the Republic’s entire field force. The States-General overruled them. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

On June 19, Maurice assembled 12,000 infantry and 1,600 cavalry — the flower of the States’ army — at Castle Rammekens. A vast fleet of warships and transports carried them to the Sas of Ghent, where they disembarked on June 22. The army marched through Flanders, capturing Oudenburg and the forts around Ostend, reaching Nieuport on July 1. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Albert’s Counter-March

Archduke Albert, roused from his delusion that danger lay on his eastern border, assembled an army from mutinous Spanish garrisons. On June 29 he reviewed 10,000 foot and nearly 2,000 cavalry at Ghent. Marching with extraordinary speed, he surprised and captured Oudenburg on July 1, butchered the garrisons of the surrounding forts, and planted himself across Maurice’s line of retreat. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

The Stand at Leffingen Bridge

At midnight on July 1-2, Maurice learned that the archduke was in force. With the tide dividing his army across Nieuport haven, he sent Count Ernest of Nassau with 2,000 men — the Scottish regiment of Edmonds and the Zeelanders of Van der Noot — to seize the bridge at Leffingen and hold the enemy at bay while the main army crossed. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Ernest reached the bridge too late; the enemy held it. He formed his troops behind a dyke and awaited the onslaught. The Spanish attacked in overwhelming numbers. Risoir’s cavalry fled without a blow, the Zeelanders panicked, and the Scots threw away their arms and drowned themselves in the sea. Within hours, a thousand of Maurice’s best troops were killed or drowned, all standards and artillery lost, and the Spanish had hardly a casualty. The archduke sent word to the Infanta that he would soon send Maurice a prisoner. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

The Battle on the Beach

Maurice received the news of Ernest’s destruction without flinching. He took a supreme decision: he ordered the entire fleet to put to sea, removing any possibility of escape. The army would conquer or die. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

By low tide, the main army crossed the haven — infantry wading with water to their armpits. The advance under Sir Francis Vere (2,600 English and 2,800 Frisians) drew up on the beach and in the downs, with a battery of six demi-cannon on the edge of the downs. The battalia under Solms and the rearguard under Tempel formed behind. Count Lewis Gunther of Nassau commanded eight squadrons of cavalry on the left flank. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

The Spanish army advanced in the intolerable July heat. For two hours the armies faced each other. At about 2 PM, the archduke gave the signal. The battle was fought in the shifting, burning sands of the downs — a desperate infantry struggle where Vere’s English and Frisians bore the brunt. Lewis Gunther’s cavalry charged brilliantly in the pastures, routing the Spanish horse, but the pursuit nearly cut him off. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

The Crisis and Rally

The infantry fight swayed back and forth for over an hour. Thrice the Spaniards were repulsed, thrice they rallied. At last, with Lewis Gunther’s cavalry broken and Vere’s English and Frisians driven back, the day seemed lost. The Spanish shouted victory. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

At that supreme moment, Maurice — immovable as a rock — led the last three reserve squadrons (Balen’s, Vere’s own, and Cecil’s) in a charge toward the battery on the beach. Balen charged along the strand, Horace Vere through the upper downs, Cecil along the margin. The Spanish infantry near the guns were broken. The Zeeland sailors manning the demi-cannon opened fire once more. At the first volley the Spaniards staggered; the sailors shouted “Ian-fall on!” — and the fugitives rallied. A final cavalry charge decided the battle. The Spanish army broke and fled. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Aftermath

Three thousand Spaniards lay dead; six hundred prisoners were taken, including the Admiral of Arragon. The States’ losses, including the morning disaster, were about 2,000. One hundred and twenty standards and seven cannon were captured. The archduke barely escaped capture at the bridge of Leffingen, riding without pause to Bruges. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Maurice fell to his knees on the sand, weeping, and exclaimed: “O God, what are we human creatures to whom Thou hast brought such honour, and to whom Thou hast vouchsafed such a victory!” ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

Three days later, Maurice returned before Nieuport — only to find the garrison strengthened. After a rainy week, he abandoned the siege. The invasion of Flanders was an absolute failure. As Motley concludes, the expedition was a grave mistake saved from annihilation only by Maurice’s heroism, Vere’s gallantry, and Ernest’s self-sacrifice. The only fruit was the moral effect of demonstrating that the Republic’s army could defeat Spanish veterans in a pitched battle on open ground. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]

The alienation between Maurice and Oldenbarnevelt, which would ultimately destroy both men, may be dated from the day of Nieuport. ^[source: motley-united-netherlands.txt]
